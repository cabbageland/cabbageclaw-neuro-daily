## Quick verdict

* Useful

Keep this as an evidence-map note, not as a treatment-efficacy note. The review finds only eight eligible clinical studies: three HIFU/MRgFUS ablation studies and five LIFU/transcranial ultrasound stimulation studies. It is useful because it separates ablative HIFU from reversible LIFU and keeps the field's weak spots visible: small samples, heterogeneous diagnoses and targets, variable follow-up, and high or unclear bias risk.

## One-paragraph overview

The review asks what human clinical evidence exists for focused ultrasound in psychiatric or neuropsychiatric disorders. It searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane for 2014-2025 studies using HIFU or LIFU as the primary intervention in adults with psychiatric conditions, then narratively summarized targets, symptoms, scales, adverse events, and bias. The evidence base is very small: 27 HIFU patients, mostly treatment-refractory OCD or MDD receiving MR-guided bilateral anterior-limb-of-internal-capsule capsulotomy, and 138 LIFU participants across mood, anxiety, trauma-related, and schizophrenia-negative-symptom studies targeting fronto-limbic regions such as right fronto-temporal cortex, amygdala, and DLPFC. Symptom scales often improved and short-term tolerability was generally favorable, but the correct conclusion is that focused ultrasound remains investigational in psychiatry and needs standardized, sham-controlled, biomarker-rich trials.

## Model definition

This is not a trainable predictive model paper. The relevant model is a systematic-review evidence map separating focused ultrasound modalities, targets, diagnoses, outcomes, and risk of bias.

### Inputs

Clinical studies of adults with psychiatric or neuropsychiatric disorders using HIFU/MRgFUS or LIFU/transcranial ultrasound stimulation as the primary intervention; study design; diagnosis; target; ultrasound parameters when available; clinical scales; adverse events; follow-up; and bias assessment.

### Outputs

A narrative synthesis of the available HIFU and LIFU psychiatry evidence, including included-study counts, target and diagnosis coverage, symptom-scale changes, adverse-event patterns, study-quality limitations, and recommended trial-design priorities.

### Training objective (loss)

There is no machine-learning loss. The review uses PRISMA-style screening, qualitative extraction, NIH quality assessment for uncontrolled HIFU studies, and Cochrane RoB 2 framing for randomized or sham-controlled LIFU studies.

### Architecture / parameterization

A systematic review registered in PROSPERO, with searches across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane. Studies were grouped by modality: HIFU/MRgFUS, where the main psychiatric use is focal ablation, and LIFU/transcranial ultrasound stimulation, where the main aim is reversible neuromodulation.

## Key questions this summary must address

### 1. What problem is the paper trying to solve?

Focused ultrasound psychiatry has a vocabulary problem and an evidence problem. HIFU and LIFU often get discussed under one exciting umbrella, even though one is usually ablative and the other is intended as reversible neuromodulation. The paper tries to summarize what human psychiatric evidence actually exists and how far it is from routine clinical use.

### 2. What is the method?

The authors searched four databases for 2014-2025 clinical studies in adults with psychiatric or neuropsychiatric disorders where focused ultrasound was the primary intervention. They included trials, pilots, open-label studies, and sham-controlled studies reporting clinical outcomes, targets, and adverse events. They excluded nonpsychiatric indications, animal studies, reviews, protocols, conference abstracts, duplicate datasets, and studies where another neuromodulation modality was primary.

### 3. What is the method motivation?

Psychiatric focused ultrasound can mean two very different things. HIFU can create focal thermal lesions for severe refractory illness, while LIFU aims to perturb circuits reversibly without tissue destruction. A sane evidence review has to keep those mechanisms separate instead of asking which generic "ultrasound" works better.

### 4. What data does it use?

Eight studies met inclusion criteria. The HIFU/MRgFUS group included three studies and 27 patients with OCD or MDD, mostly targeting the anterior limb of the internal capsule. The LIFU group included five studies and 138 participants across mild to moderate depression, treatment-resistant generalized anxiety disorder, schizophrenia with negative symptoms, major depression, and mood/anxiety/trauma-related disorders. Targets included right fronto-temporal cortex, right amygdala, DLPFC, and left amygdala.

### 5. How is it evaluated?

The review evaluates studies narratively because the diagnoses, targets, parameters, outcome scales, and follow-up windows were too heterogeneous for meta-analysis. It summarizes changes in clinical scales, adverse events, study designs, and risk of bias. HIFU studies were judged with the NIH before-after quality tool because they were uncontrolled. LIFU studies were assessed with RoB 2 where randomized or sham-controlled designs allowed it.

### 6. What are the main results?

For HIFU/MRgFUS, all three included psychiatric studies used ALIC capsulotomy logic. Reported changes included Y-BOCS reductions around 11 to 13 points in OCD samples, HAM-D reductions around 7 to 14 points, and common adverse effects such as headache and vestibular symptoms. For LIFU, the review reports mixed but often favorable symptom-scale movement: one right fronto-temporal cortex depression pilot did not clearly move BDI-II, while other studies reported improvements in anxiety, schizophrenia negative symptoms, depression, suicidality, mood state, general distress, anxiety severity, and anhedonia. Across modalities, adverse events were usually mild and transient, commonly headache, dizziness, scalp sensitivity, irritability, tingling, or procedure discomfort, with no serious neurological or psychiatric complications reported in the summarized trials.

### 7. What is actually novel?

The novelty is not a new experiment. The useful contribution is a compact clinical-evidence map that explicitly separates HIFU as an ablative psychiatric neurosurgery tool from LIFU as a reversible circuit-modulation tool. That distinction matters because safety, ethics, patient selection, target validation, and trial design differ radically across the two.

### 8. What are the strengths?

The review is full-text accessible, PRISMA-framed, PROSPERO-registered, and cautious about HIFU/LIFU comparison. It extracts target, diagnosis, parameter, scale, adverse-event, and bias information rather than only listing symptom effects. It also states the correct implementation conclusion: focused ultrasound psychiatry belongs in specialized research settings until larger randomized and sham-controlled trials define efficacy, safety, targets, and clinical role.

### 9. What are the weaknesses, limitations, or red flags?

The underlying evidence base is tiny and messy. HIFU studies are open-label and uncontrolled; LIFU studies vary by diagnosis, target, dose, device, follow-up, and outcome scale. The review cannot do a quantitative meta-analysis. Some table labeling and attribution are awkward enough that this should be treated as a map back to primary studies, not as the final quantitative source. The symptom improvements are also vulnerable to expectancy, regression, selection, and short follow-up, especially when target engagement is not measured.

### 10. What challenges or open problems remain?

The field still needs standardized acoustic dosing, prospective skull and target modeling, sham controls that actually match the subjective experience, longer follow-up, systematic adverse-event capture, and explicit biomarkers of target engagement. It also needs to decide which psychiatric indications justify irreversible HIFU lesions versus reversible LIFU perturbation.

### 11. What future work naturally follows?

The right next generation is not another small open-label symptom series. It is multicenter randomized sham-controlled trials with prespecified targets, harmonized scales, acoustic-field documentation, neuroimaging or physiological target-engagement readouts, and follow-up long enough to distinguish durable therapeutic change from short-term state effects.

### 12. Why does this matter for cabbageland?

It gives the focused-ultrasound psychiatry lane a reality check. The field has enough human signal to stay interesting, especially around fronto-limbic LIFU and ALIC HIFU, but not enough controlled evidence to treat "deep noninvasive" as a solved mechanism. It is a useful companion to the amygdala and subcallosal-cingulate target-engagement notes because it shows how thin the broader clinical scaffold still is.

### 13. What ideas are steal-worthy?

Separate modality from mechanism: ablative HIFU and reversible LIFU should not be collapsed into one treatment class. Use systematic reviews to identify missing trial primitives, not to launder weak primary studies into confidence. Demand target-engagement measures before symptom claims get promoted. Treat short-term tolerability as necessary but not remotely sufficient for psychiatric implementation.

### 14. Final decision

Keep as a map and guardrail. It is not the strongest individual science paper in the archive, but it is useful for framing focused ultrasound psychiatry honestly: promising, early, heterogeneous, and still in need of serious target-engagement and sham-controlled evidence.
