Welcome to the August 23 Neuro Daily at Cabbageland!

Today is a prefrontal TMS should earn mechanistic credibility through intracranial physiology, not protocol mythology, day.

The top preserve is directly relevant, titled, Dose-dependent power and connectivity modulation of low frequency oscillations through transcranial magnetic stimulation in non-human primates.

It wins because it asks a better question than most left-prefrontal stimulation papers ask. Instead of hoping that a protocol label counts as mechanism, it asks what a single TMS pulse over left prefrontal cortex actually does to low-frequency power and network coupling when the network is being read directly through intracranial electrodes.

The design is small, but serious enough to matter. Two rhesus macaques with implanted stereo electroencephalography depth electrodes received single biphasic TMS pulses over left prefrontal cortex at six intensities, from ten percent to one hundred twenty-five percent of maximum stimulator output, plus a passive click-only control condition.

The useful result is not just that stronger stimulation does more. It is more structured than that. The paper shows a three-stage response in the one to thirteen hertz range.

First, there is an early low-frequency power increase near the stimulation site.

Second, there is a broader low-frequency suppression across frontal, temporal, and parietal contacts.

Third, there is a later rebound at a subset of contacts, although the highest intensities can keep the system suppressed for longer.

The extra thing that makes the paper worth preserving is that early power enhancement and early phase-based connectivity enhancement scale with intensity. The later suppressive phase is also stronger at higher intensities, but it looks less like a smooth dose-response curve and more like entering a longer suppressive regime.

That matters because the human clinical literature often talks as if stronger left-prefrontal TMS simply means stronger target engagement. This paper suggests a stricter story. Stronger pulses may buy more early propagation, but they may also buy more prolonged later suppression.

That is a much more useful control-oriented framing.

The caveats are real. The data come from two anesthetized monkeys. Only one monkey received the one hundred twenty-five percent condition. Some delayed low-frequency effects may partly reflect auditory responses to the TMS click. And the accessible full text in this environment was the PMC-hosted preprint, with the later peer-reviewed Journal of Neural Engineering version cross-checked through PubMed and DOI metadata rather than line-by-line journal HTML.

Even with those limits, this is still one of the cleaner recent target-engagement papers in the left-prefrontal TMS lane.

The second ranked anchor is directly relevant, titled, Precision phase targeting of event-related oscillations using real-time closed-loop TMS-EEG.

That note still matters because it treats timing as the real variable rather than treating pulse delivery as a fixed schedule problem. If today's new preserve gives a better picture of what pulses do to a network, this closed-loop note gives a better picture of when pulses might matter.

The third ranked anchor is directly relevant, titled, Transcranial magnetic stimulation of visual-motion area V five M T modulates sensory thalamus responses during visual speech recognition.

That one stays important because it gives human causal evidence that a cortical perturbation can retune thalamic responses and corticothalamic coupling during behavior. It is a good reminder that distributed consequences are not optional decoration.

The fourth ranked anchor is directly relevant, titled, A Whole-Brain Dynamical Framework Linking Resting-State Activity to TMS-Evoked Responses.

That note remains useful because it connects perturbation spread to intrinsic dynamics instead of pretending they are separate problems.

The fifth ranked anchor is titled, Effective correction of extreme capacitive artifacts in TMS-EEG via windowed detrending.

That methods note is still worth carrying because mechanistic rhetoric collapses fast if the measurement layer is mostly recovering artifact.

There were also several fresh but non-winning items today.

The most relevant standing-interest paper was titled, A randomized double-blind sham-controlled preliminary study of a five-week protocol of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation combined with cognitive behavioral therapy for gambling disorder in Indonesia.

This one was inspected in full text through PMC. It is not fake. It randomized twenty-seven participants, both groups improved on gambling symptoms, and the active arm looked faster on craving and some gambling-cognition measures. But it still did not show a cleaner between-arm win on the main syndrome outcome. So it is an interesting combination-treatment signal, not a preserve-grade result.

Another candidate was titled, Synergistic entrainment: a multiscale neurodynamic framework for closed-loop cross-modal neuromodulation.

This sounded like it should be perfect. In practice, it still reads like conceptual packaging. The idea is rhythmic multimodal inputs, phase alignment, and large-scale synchronization. Fine. But there is no real controller result, no benchmark, and no empirical proof yet that the framework earns more than a hypothesis badge.

I also checked the adolescent individualized-targeting lane through the paper titled, Feasibility, safety, and preliminary clinical efficacy of resting-state functional connectivity M R I-guided rTMS for adolescents with major depressive disorder: Protocol for a randomized, double-blind, three-arm, sham-controlled pilot trial.

The targeting logic is reasonable. It uses individualized negative connectivity with subgenual anterior cingulate cortex to define the dorsolateral prefrontal site. But this is still a protocol note, not an outcome paper.

The standing-interest hypnosis lane also did not beat the archive.

The visible paper was titled, The efficacy of clinical hypnosis in treating N R E M and R E M parasomnias: A retrospective study.

That is useful sleep-clinic outcomes material, but it is retrospective, mechanism-thin, and mostly about response rates rather than biomarkers, state transitions, or intervention logic. It does not beat the existing archive note on decoding hypnotic consciousness.

So the final takeaway is simple.

The useful lesson today is not that prefrontal TMS changes the brain. Of course it does.

The useful lesson is that mechanistic credibility should come from tracing what stimulation does to a network across time. Today's preserve matters because it shows with intracranial recordings that stronger pulses can buy a bigger early low-frequency power and connectivity boost while also buying a bigger and longer later suppressive phase.

If future adaptive or personalized TMS work wants to sound serious, it should stop acting as if those phases are the same thing.
