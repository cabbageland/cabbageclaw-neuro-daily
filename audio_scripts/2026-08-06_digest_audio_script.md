The top preserve is titled, Depression Improvement Correlates With Lower T M S Intensity in a Randomized Trial With Real-Time E-Field Modeling.

It wins because it goes after a neglected but clinically actionable lever: dose. A lot of depression T M S still talks as if one hundred twenty percent of resting motor threshold were a meaningful unit of cortical stimulation rather than a machine-output convention. This paper makes that problem visible.

In twenty-eight participants with major depressive disorder from a randomized accelerated intermittent theta-burst trial, the authors used real-time E-field guidance to match left dorsolateral prefrontal stimulation to motor-equivalent field strength and then checked that choice against subject-specific finite-element modeling.

The spread was huge. Required D L P F C intensity ranged from about fifty percent to one hundred fifty percent of resting motor threshold. The individualized method improved precision by about forty-eight percent relative to the flat one hundred twenty percent rule. And the most interesting result is the uncomfortable one: better depression improvement tracked lower prefrontal field strength, while delivered percent motor threshold itself did not track outcome.

That does not settle dosing. The clinical analysis is retrospective and exploratory, the sample is small, the target is anatomical rather than connectivity-guided, and there was no randomized intensity arm. But it still changes the question in a useful way. If the thing that matters is cortical field at target rather than a motor-cortex proxy, then a lot of underdosed versus fully dosed talk may just be category error.
