Welcome to the August fifteenth Neuro Daily at Cabbageland!

Today is an activity-weighted spread beats anatomy-only connectomics day.

The top preserve is directly relevant, titled, Activity-dependent epidemic spreading on multiscale brain networks predicts Alzheimer's disease progression.

It wins because it asks a better question than most pathology-spread papers ask. Instead of saying tau follows the connectome and calling it a day, it asks how neuronal activity changes whether pathology can take off at all, and where it should go first if it does. That turns disease progression into a state-dependent network problem rather than a static wiring-diagram problem.

The core setup is clear. The authors couple a node-activity process to susceptible-infected-susceptible spreading on a structural brain network. That gives them two useful objects. The first is an activity-dependent epidemic threshold, which says how easy it is for a small pathological seed to grow. The second is an activity-dependent dominant eigenmode, which says where growth should concentrate first.

The paper is not content to leave that as elegant math. It tests the framework against longitudinal human imaging. The structural backbone is a group connectome. Regional glucose metabolism from F D G P E T serves as a proxy for neuronal activity. Tau P E T provides the future pathology outcome. The datasets come from A D N I, H A B S, and A four.

The strongest results are regional rather than individual. Activity-dependent correction terms and fitted dominant modes remain associated with later tau accumulation even after adjustment for anatomy-only structure, P E T inputs, and amyloid. The partial correlations are not tiny. The first-order and second-order regional corrections land around zero point four four, minus zero point four three, zero point three nine, and zero point four four. The fitted dominant modes land around zero point four five to zero point four seven.

The threshold result is weaker, but still useful. In the lower-to-moderate amyloid range, specifically the centiloid below fifty subgroup, easier predicted spreading is associated with broader tau extent. The effect is modest, around rho equals zero point two one nine. But that is still directionally interesting because threshold logic should matter most near the transition into widespread spread, not once pathology is already everywhere.

The important caveat is that this is not a patient-specific intervention engine. The connectome is group-averaged. F D G is only a proxy for neuronal activity. Within-region variation is inferred rather than measured directly. And the nicest threshold result depends on the observed best centiloid cutoff, so those p-values are nominal rather than something to posture over.

Still, it is worth keeping because it upgrades the vocabulary. The useful message is that pathology may follow structure as modulated by activity and hidden multiscale variation, not structure alone.

The second ranked anchor is titled, Trophic structure predicts seizure propagation in brain network models.

It stays high because it sharpens seizure-network reasoning around directed hierarchy and cycle structure instead of generic hub folklore.

The third ranked anchor is titled, Literature-Guided Minimax Optimization of Virtual Epilepsy Neurostimulation.

It stays important because it asks the right optimization question. Protect the patient who fails first, not the average case that flatters the paper.

The fourth ranked anchor is titled, Depression Improvement Correlates With Lower T M S Intensity in a Randomized Trial With Real-Time E-Field Modeling.

There were other fresh papers today, but they did not beat the preserve. One was the NeuroImage paper titled, Interictal thalamocortical network configuration is associated with susceptibility to seizure-related impaired consciousness in focal epilepsy. It is clinically relevant and the centromedian-thalamic framing is real, but the readable full body text stayed blocked in this environment, so confidence stayed abstract-level.

Another was the Brain Stimulation paper titled, Minimal Reservoir Computing Generates Hippocampal-Inspired Stimulation for Open-Loop Modulation of Cortical Networks In Vitro. The idea is genuinely interesting. A tiny reservoir generates a low-event irregular schedule, and the in vitro modulation signal is not nothing. But the accessible evidence stayed too thin, and the result is still heterogeneous open-loop proof of concept rather than a convincing control paper.

Another was the Journal of Neural Engineering paper titled, Endovascular neuromodulation: Feasibility of endovascular stimulation near the cerebellum. That is a nice platform-methods signal, but publisher anti-bot barriers again kept the read too shallow for a canonical preserve.

The main takeaway is simple. Structure alone is probably too thin a story for pathological spread. Today’s preserve matters because it couples neuronal activity to network epidemic dynamics and shows that the resulting threshold and eigenmode language predicts longitudinal human tau progression better than anatomy-only structure does. It is still a group-level early-disease model, not a patient-specific control system. But it gives a much sharper way to think about when pathology takes off and where it goes next.
