The top preserve is titled, Persistent homology broadens the controllable subspace in human structural connectomes.

It wins because it goes straight at a stale habit in brain network control. Too much of this literature still ranks candidate driver nodes by degree-like strength and then lets a scalar energy summary pretend to be the whole control problem. This paper shows why that shortcut is too thin.

Across seventy healthy-adult diffusion MRI connectomes at sixty-eight, one hundred fourteen, and two hundred nineteen parcels, the authors compare the usual degree-based driver ranking with a topology-informed ranking based on persistent H one cycle participation. Average control energy barely changes, only about zero point two percent. But the topology-informed sets consistently broaden the controllable subspace, raise effective rank and participation ratio, lower condition number, stay more resilient under simulated hub loss, and become cheaper for visually weighted target states.

That is the real contribution. The useful quantity is not just whether a driver set is cheap on average. It is what directions of state space that set opens up, how anisotropic the reach is, and what classes of targets become easier or harder. The paper also makes a broader methodological point worth keeping. As parcellation gets finer, the scalar energy landscape compresses enough that node identity matters less and less for average energy. So tiny energy differences at fine parcellation should not be trusted as if they settled the targeting question.
