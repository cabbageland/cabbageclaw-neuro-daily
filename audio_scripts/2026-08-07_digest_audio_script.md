The top preserve is titled, Modelling temporal dynamics of suicidal ideation and behaviour across pre- to early adolescence using a Markov framework.

It wins because it takes a domain that is usually flattened into generic risk labels and makes it legible as a state-transition problem with age dependence and uncertainty. Using child self-report A B C D data from eleven thousand eight hundred sixty-four participants across ages nine to thirteen, the authors define eight states spanning passive ideation, active ideation, suicidal behaviour, and whether non-suicidal self-injury, or N S S I, is also present. They then estimate year-specific transition matrices, multi-year transition probabilities, first-passage probabilities, bootstrap confidence intervals, and entropy-based uncertainty.

The useful part is not magical prediction. The useful part is that remission, escalation, persistence, and volatility can all be discussed in one explicit language. Remission to a no-report state is still the most common next-year outcome for most starting states, but remission declines with age and with more advanced states. N S S I consistently pushes trajectories toward higher risk and lower predictability. By age eleven to twelve, active ideation with N S S I carries about a six percent next-year probability of suicidal behaviour, compared with about four percent without N S S I. A child with passive ideation at age nine to ten has roughly a seven point eight percent three-year probability of reaching suicidal behaviour, rising to about eleven percent when N S S I is co-reported.

The caveats are real. This is annual self-report data. Current and lifetime indicators are aggregated in a way that creates temporal ambiguity. The model is first-order Markov, cohort-level, and descriptive rather than individualized prediction. There is no treatment variable, no neurobiology inside the state model, and no causal identification. But for developmental psychiatry and interventional psychiatry, a model that keeps volatility visible is already a meaningful upgrade over static risk-factor sludge.

The second ranked anchor is titled, Latent class modeling of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation response trajectories in treatment-resistant depression.

It stays high because it keeps the interventional psychiatry side honest. Treatment response is not one clean endpoint. It is a trajectory-class problem, and some patients likely need an early pivot away from a failing acute course.

The third ranked anchor is titled, Common Electrophysiology Biomarkers Collected at Home Robustly Track Depression Recovery With Deep Brain Stimulation.

It remains important because it treats psychiatric recovery as a slow-timescale state-estimation problem rather than mood-scale folklore.

The fourth ranked anchor is titled, A Whole-Brain Dynamical Framework Linking Resting-State Activity to T M S-Evoked Responses.

It is still a good archive piece because it keeps intrinsic dynamics and perturbational response inside one explicit model instead of pretending those are separate stories.

The main takeaway is simple. Volatility is not just noise around risk. Today’s preserve matters because it shows that N S S I changes not only the average direction of early-adolescent suicidal trajectories but also how predictable those trajectories are. That does not make a first-order Markov chain the final answer. It does make static risk framing look too blunt for the problem.
