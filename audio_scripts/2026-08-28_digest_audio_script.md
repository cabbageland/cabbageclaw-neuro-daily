Short overview
The top fresh preserve today is Comparing transcranial magnetic stimulation and esketamine treatment response trajectories in resistant depression. It matters because interventional psychiatry keeps smearing together two different questions. One is whether a treatment can work at all. The other is how fast it buys relief when a patient is severely depressed or carrying suicidal burden. This paper does not solve treatment selection causally, but it does answer the timing question with enough discipline to be worth keeping. In a 372-patient UCSD treatment-resistant depression cohort, intranasal esketamine separates early from standard real-world rTMS: median antidepressant response arrives at 36 versus 49 days, restricted mean survival time favors esketamine by about 11.9 days over 90 days, and suicidal-ideation improvement arrives at 9 versus 26 days. By roughly 90 days, though, the response curves converge enough that the honest read is speed advantage, not proven superiority.
That speed-versus-superiority distinction gets sharper when paired with the archive note Latent class modeling of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation response trajectories in treatment-resistant depression, which I refreshed today from full-text PMC access instead of the older abstract-only read. That paper still matters because it shows the clinically annoying object more clearly than most rTMS biomarker chatter does: a high-baseline, minimal-improvement lane that stays bad while another equally severe group improves a lot. Put together, the two papers suggest something practical and severe. If a high-severity patient on rTMS is not moving and carries suicidal burden, the field should stop pretending that waiting politely is neutral.
The nearby fresh rTMS symptom-domain preprint, Changes in Depressive Symptom Domains During Treatment with Accelerated and Conventional Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation, did not clear preserve threshold today. The abstract-level result is that accelerated 5x5 rTMS and conventional once-daily rTMS improve fourteen symptom domains similarly, with only exploratory time-course differences on anxiety, rumination, mood, depression, and vigor. That is interesting, but the paper stays too pooled and too under-inspected here to beat the stronger sequencing and trajectory notes.
The standing-interest checks stayed below preserve threshold again. No fresh network or computational paper I could inspect today beat the archive's whole-brain TMS dynamics anchor.

Most relevant paper
Comparing transcranial magnetic stimulation and esketamine treatment response trajectories in resistant depression
This is the most relevant paper today because it does something boring and useful that the field keeps dodging. It compares two approved interventional psychiatry options on the time axis that patients actually live through. The right question is not only "which one can produce response?" but "what happens in the first month when the person in front of you is miserable, maybe suicidal, and cannot afford a lot of ceremonial waiting?"
The answer here is clear enough to matter. Esketamine separates earlier. In this UCSD cohort, median response happens at 36 days with esketamine and 49 days with rTMS, and median suicidal-ideation improvement happens at 9 versus 26 days. The weighted survival analyses and sensitivity checks do enough work that this is not just one careless retrospective comparison. But the paper is also disciplined enough to avoid the dumber conclusion. Overall response and remission rates are not so different that esketamine gets to call itself the better treatment in any settled sense, and the curves converge later.
That distinction matters because it changes how the paper should be used. It is not a modality supremacy paper. It is a timing paper. For high-acuity cases, earlier relief is a real clinical property. For broader treatment strategy, the result mostly says that standard real-world rTMS may be slower, not that it is obsolete.
The paper also earns usefulness by admitting what confounds it. rTMS pooled multiple protocols. PHQ-9 assessments were denser in esketamine care. Prior rTMS exposure was much more common in the esketamine arm. Those are not minor details. They are exactly why the right interpretation is narrowed to observed timing in this care structure rather than elevated into grand causal treatment-selection doctrine.
Novelty / framing / baseline impact
For interventional psychiatry framing: this paper cleanly separates early relief from endpoint superiority, which the field too often treats as the same thing.
For sequencing logic: it strengthens the case that severe or suicidal patients may need earlier pivot reasoning instead of passive completion of a failing standard rTMS course.
For rTMS heterogeneity work: the refreshed latent-class note is the right companion because it shows which rTMS patients may be on the bad lane that a faster alternative should be judged against.
For accelerated-rTMS comparison papers: today's abstract-only symptom-domain preprint does not overturn the timing lesson because it is still too pooled and too lightly inspectable.
For CBT-plus-interventional combinations: the small gambling-disorder rTMS-plus-CBT trial still does not beat the archive meta-analytic anchor on useful implementation logic.
For hypnosis and hypnotherapy: today's parasomnia cohort remains clinically respectable but not more mechanistically useful than the archive hypnosis state-change anchor.
For computational and network framing: no fresh inspectable paper today beat the archive whole-brain TMS dynamics note as a control-legible account of perturbation spread.
One-paragraph takeaway
The useful August 28 lesson is that time is part of treatment logic. Today's preserve matters because it shows that intranasal esketamine buys earlier symptom and suicidal-ideation relief than standard real-world rTMS in a serious TRD cohort, while stopping short of pretending that faster means categorically better. Paired with the trajectory paper, the practical implication is sharper than average-outcome rhetoric usually allows: if a high-severity rTMS patient is not moving early, the burden of proof should shift toward why you are still waiting.
