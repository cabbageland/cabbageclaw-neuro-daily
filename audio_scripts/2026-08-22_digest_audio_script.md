Today is a fixed-frequency stimulation only matters if it moves the endogenous rhythm day.

The top preserve is directly relevant, titled, Suppression of endogenous alpha power predicts clinical response to ten hertz transcranial alternating current stimulation in major depressive disorder: A double-blind randomized controlled trial.

It wins because it asks a much better question than the average depression stimulation paper.

The lazy version of this literature goes like this. A protocol says ten hertz. Symptoms drift a little. Someone waves at alpha oscillations and calls the job done.

This paper makes that shortcut compete against something stricter. It asks whether the clinically relevant physiology lives at the applied stimulation frequency, or in the brain's own endogenous alpha rhythm.

The design is modest but serious enough to matter. Twenty adults with major depressive disorder were randomized to five consecutive days of active or sham bifrontal-central ten hertz transcranial alternating current stimulation. Resting one hundred twenty-eight channel electroencephalography was collected before treatment on day one, before treatment on day five, and again at two-week follow-up.

Now for the useful part. The group-level symptom result is not clean enough to carry the whole paper. Hamilton Depression scores and self-reported depression scores improved over time in both groups. The active arm looked numerically better, but there was no significant time-by-condition interaction and no response or remission advantage over sham.

That sounds disappointing only if you wanted another flimsy efficacy headline.

What makes the paper worth preserving is the target-engagement story. Inside the active condition, larger reductions in alpha power at each participant's individual alpha frequency predicted larger later improvement in depression symptoms. That happened both for early suppression during the intervention week and for sustained suppression by follow-up.

The important contrast is that the same thing did not happen for generic ten hertz power.

So the paper's useful dissociation is this. The nominal stimulation frequency leaves one kind of footprint, but the clinically relevant signal seems to live in how well the intervention perturbs the endogenous alpha system.

There is also a second result that keeps the paper from collapsing into a simple personalization slogan. The clearest stimulation-frequency effect was a delayed posterior cluster of reduced ten hertz power at follow-up, not a simple local prefrontal story. So the paper does not prove that matching stimulation to each person's alpha frequency is already solved. It says something narrower and better. The protocol label and the clinically useful physiology are not the same thing.

That is exactly the kind of framing improvement this literature needs.

The caveats are real. The sample is only twenty people. One active participant was lost to follow-up. The sham group had longer current depressive episodes and more psychotherapy exposure at baseline. The electroencephalography remains sensor-space rather than anatomically direct. And the strongest full-text access in this environment came from the open medRxiv preprint, with the final journal abstract cross-checked through PubMed.

Even with those caveats, the paper earns the keep because it gives future depression tACS work a better mechanistic standard. If you want to sound serious about target engagement, you should show what endogenous rhythm actually tracks improvement, not just what number was printed on the stimulator.

The second ranked anchor is directly relevant, titled, Differential effects of individualized vs. fixed-frequency transcranial alternating current stimulation on clinical and electroencephalography outcomes in major depressive disorder.

That archive note still matters because it is the clean skepticism check on premature personalization claims. It reminds us that individualized frequency does not automatically beat a simple fixed ten hertz protocol just because it sounds more precise.

The third ranked anchor is directly relevant, titled, A Whole-Brain Dynamical Framework Linking Resting-State Activity to TMS-Evoked Responses.

That note remains valuable because it gives the network-computational version of the same broader lesson. Perturbation is easier to understand when it is tied to intrinsic dynamics instead of treated as a disconnected intervention.

The fourth ranked anchor is directly relevant, titled, Phase- and amplitude-dependent control of synchronization in excitatory-inhibitory networks via pulsed stimulation.

That one stays useful because it sharpens control logic. The same pulse can synchronize, barely perturb, or desynchronize a network depending on timing and amplitude. It is still one of the better recent notes on why stimulation phase should be treated as a real control variable rather than an aesthetic add-on.

The fifth ranked anchor is titled, Effective correction of extreme capacitive artifacts in TMS-EEG via windowed detrending.

That methods note remains important because all the target-engagement rhetoric in the world means nothing if the sensing stack is mostly recovering artifact.

There were also two fresh comparison papers that did not win today.

The first was the Journal of Neuroscience paper titled, Real-Time Brain-State-Coupled Corticocortical Paired Associative Stimulation of Cognitive Networks.

Directionally, it is in exactly the right family. Real-time state coupling and cognitive network stimulation are both worth caring about. But the accessible text today was abstract-level only, which is not enough to inspect the online state-detection logic, stimulation contingency, or effect geometry with confidence.

The second was the paper titled, Nonlinear neurodynamics of N2 sleep electroencephalography predict outcomes of anterior nucleus of the thalamus deep brain stimulation in epilepsy: a pilot study.

That one is also directionally relevant. A twenty-six patient nonlinear sleep electroencephalography predictor for deep brain stimulation outcome could become useful later. But at abstract level it still reads more like promising pilot classifier packaging than a preserve-grade mechanistic note.

I also checked the standing-interest lanes.

In hypnosis and hypnotherapy, the visible review was titled, Hypnosis on Perioperative Outcomes Among Patients Undergoing Non-Cardiovascular Surgeries: A Systematic Review of Randomized Trials.

That is mostly perioperative anxiety and medication-use literature. It may be useful in anesthesia-adjunct terms, but it is not a stronger hypnosis mechanism or state-transition paper than the archive note on decoding hypnotic consciousness.

In the cognitive behavioral therapy plus interventional psychiatry lane, the main visible paper was titled, Does mental health coaching improve efficacy of transcranial magnetic stimulation for major depression? A pilot randomized controlled trial and benchmarking study.

Interesting services-layer material, but still not a winner. It randomized thirty-six adults to coaching-supported digital cognitive behavioral therapy versus coaching-supported narrative content during a six-week repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation course, found no randomized-arm Hamilton Depression advantage, and leaned on exploratory benchmarking against a nonrandomized sample for any stronger story.

So the final takeaway is simple.

The useful lesson today is not that ten hertz transcranial alternating current stimulation works. It is that fixed-frequency stimulation only starts to look mechanistically serious when it shows what endogenous rhythm it actually changes. Today's preserve matters because it finds that later depression improvement tracks suppression of each participant's individual alpha frequency rather than generic ten hertz power change.

It is still a small underpowered trial, not a decisive efficacy result.

But it gives future neuromodulation papers a much better standard for what target engagement should mean.
