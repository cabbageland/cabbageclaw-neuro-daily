The top fresh preserve is titled, Stimulus-Evoked Network Dynamics in Human Cortical Organoids: From a Graph-Computational Framework to Repeated-Stimulation Depression.

It wins because it does something rare and useful. It starts with a graph-propagation story, builds machinery for that story, and then gives up the story when the data refuse to cooperate. After the authors recover the true acquisition rate and the real stimulus timing, the response turns out to be a near-synchronous network burst with essentially no latency increase as you move away from the stimulation line. In plain language, the tissue is not showing a neat outward-traveling wave. It is bursting together. That means the propagation-depth and reachability-flavored metrics they prepared do not actually apply. Good. That negative result is more valuable than another paper that quietly forces graph language onto data that do not earn it.

The positive result is narrower and better. Two organoids were stimulated repeatedly across days one, two, three, four, and seven. A third organoid was left stimulation-naive until day seven. By that point, the repeatedly stimulated organoids had collapsed to about ten percent responsive electrodes, while the age-matched first-stimulation control still engaged about ninety-three percent of the array. The paper also shows that the first response of the day is relatively preserved while the session average collapses. So the network does not mainly lose the ability to mount one strong response. It loses endurance across repeated within-session stimulation. That is a much more specific and useful failure mode.
