## Short overview

The preserve today is the Brain Stimulation paper **Suppression of endogenous alpha power predicts clinical response to 10 Hz tACS in major depressive disorder: A double-blind randomized controlled trial**. It wins because it asks a much better mechanistic question than the average depression stimulation paper. The usual lazy story is that a protocol delivers 10 Hz stimulation, the symptoms drift a bit, and everyone pretends the protocol label itself is the biology. This paper tests whether the clinically relevant effect lives at the applied frequency or in the brain's own endogenous alpha rhythm.

The design is modest but serious enough to matter. Twenty adults with major depressive disorder were randomized to five consecutive days of active or sham bifrontal-central 10 Hz transcranial alternating current stimulation, with 128-channel resting EEG collected before treatment on Day 1, before treatment on Day 5, and again at two-week follow-up. The most important result is also the least slogan-friendly one. The paper does not show a clean group-level symptom knockout. HDRS-17 and BDI-II improved over time in both groups, with numerically larger improvement in the active arm, but no significant time-by-condition interaction and no response or remission advantage over sham.

That is exactly why the paper is useful. The mechanistic layer is better than the raw symptom layer. Within the active condition, larger reductions in alpha power at each participant's individual alpha frequency, not at generic 10 Hz power, predicted larger later HDRS improvement. Early IAF suppression during the intervention week predicted follow-up improvement, and sustained IAF suppression by follow-up did too. Meanwhile, the paper's clearest stimulation-frequency effect was a delayed posterior cluster of reduced 10 Hz power at follow-up. In other words, the nominal 10 Hz protocol still seems less informative than whether the intervention actually perturbed the endogenous alpha system in a clinically meaningful way.

That is a much better framing for future precision neuromodulation work. Fixed-frequency stimulation may still work, but not because the protocol name is magical. It may work when it effectively couples to the dominant intrinsic rhythm that matters for that person. This paper does not prove individualized stimulation is solved, and it does not prove closed-loop control is already necessary. But it does make simple protocol branding feel underdressed.

The caveats are real and they keep the paper honest. The sample is only 20 participants, one active participant was lost to follow-up, the sham group had longer current depressive episodes and more psychotherapy exposure at baseline, and the EEG claims remain sensor-space rather than anatomically direct. The paper also had stronger access on the full medRxiv preprint than on the final journal HTML in this environment, though the final PubMed abstract matched the core result pattern.

The fresh comparison lane had two directionally interesting papers that still lost on inspection depth. **Real-Time Brain-State-Coupled Corticocortical Paired Associative Stimulation of Cognitive Networks** is clearly in the right family for adaptive stimulation logic, but today's accessible text was abstract-level only, which is not enough to inspect how the online state detection and stimulation contingency actually behave. **Nonlinear neurodynamics of N2 sleep EEG predict outcomes of anterior nucleus of the thalamus deep brain stimulation in epilepsy: a pilot study** is also directionally relevant because it uses nonlinear sleep EEG features to predict DBS response, but at abstract level it still reads like promising pilot classifier packaging on 26 patients rather than a preserve-grade mechanistic note.

The archive anchors help keep the day balanced. **Differential effects of individualized vs. fixed-frequency tACS on clinical and EEG outcomes in major depressive disorder** remains the better skepticism-preserving check on whether frequency personalization is already clinically earned. **A Whole-Brain Dynamical Framework Linking Resting-State Activity to TMS-Evoked Responses** remains the cleaner bridge from intrinsic dynamics to perturbational spread. **Phase- and amplitude-dependent control of synchronization in excitatory-inhibitory networks via pulsed stimulation** is still the sharper control note on why timing alone can flip the sign of a pulse's effect. **Effective correction of extreme capacitive artifacts in TMS-EEG via windowed detrending** remains important because target-engagement rhetoric is fake if the measurement layer is artifact theater.

It does not beat **Decoding hypnotic consciousness: neural and experiential insights into induced and ideomotor suggestions**. In the **CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry** lane, the visible paper **Does mental health coaching improve efficacy of transcranial magnetic stimulation for major depression? A pilot randomized controlled trial and benchmarking study** is interesting services-layer material, but it randomized only 36 adults to iCBT-plus-coaching versus coaching-plus-narratives during rTMS, found no between-arm HDRS advantage, and relied on exploratory benchmarking against a nonrandomized sample for any stronger story. That is not enough to beat the existing implementation anchor **Noninvasive brain stimulation combined with evidence-based psychotherapy for psychiatric disorders: A meta-analysis of optimal implementation parameters**.

So the useful August 22 lesson is not "10 Hz tACS works." It is stricter than that. If a fixed-frequency stimulation protocol wants to sound mechanistically serious, it should show whether it actually perturbs the endogenous rhythm that tracks improvement, not just whether the nominal stimulation frequency leaves a statistical footprint somewhere on the scalp.

## Most relevant paper

**Suppression of endogenous alpha power predicts clinical response to 10 Hz tACS in major depressive disorder: A double-blind randomized controlled trial**

The paper matters because it refuses the usual sloppy equivalence between applied stimulation frequency and clinically relevant physiology. A lot of depression tACS work still talks as if "10 Hz protocol" and "alpha target engagement" are basically the same thing. This paper makes them compete. The better signal ends up belonging to individual alpha frequency suppression, not to generic 10 Hz power change.

That is what makes the negative-looking parts of the paper useful rather than disappointing. Group-level symptom change is not strong enough to win on its own. Both active and sham groups improved, and response or remission rates did not separate. If the paper had stopped there, it would mostly be another underpowered clinical signal. But it keeps going and shows that in the active condition only, early and sustained suppression of endogenous alpha power predicts larger later HDRS improvement. That is a real mechanistic clue.

The delayed posterior 10 Hz effect is also interesting because it stops the paper from collapsing into a simple personalization slogan. The stimulation-frequency footprint did appear, but it was delayed and topographically posterior rather than a clean local prefrontal story. So the paper does not prove "stimulate at each person's IAF and everything is solved." It says something narrower and better: the clinically relevant part of the physiology may not be identical to the protocol's nominal frequency tag.

The limitations are exactly why this stays a preserve rather than a victory lap. The sample is tiny. Baseline imbalance exists. The EEG remains sensor-space. The clinical result is numerically favorable rather than decisively significant. But the paper still earns preservation because it tightens the standard for what should count as target engagement in depression tACS.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For target-engagement logic:** fixed 10 Hz stimulation should not be treated as the biological endpoint; endogenous-alpha engagement may be the more meaningful variable.
- **For clinical-trial framing:** a weak between-group symptom result can still be mechanistically useful if the physiology-outcome relationship is condition-specific and well constrained.
- **For personalization claims:** this paper supports individualized or state-dependent future designs, but only as a hypothesis sharpened by mechanism, not as a victory already won.
- **For network interpretation:** delayed posterior 10 Hz suppression plus frontal and parietal IAF correlations point toward broader network reorganization rather than a simple local entrainment story.
- **For fresh citation material:** the abstract-level PAS and ANT-DBS biomarker papers are directionally relevant, but they still need fuller method access before they deserve preserve status.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 22 lesson is that fixed-frequency neuromodulation only starts to look mechanistically serious when it shows what endogenous rhythm it actually changes. Today's preserve matters because it separates the protocol label from the clinically relevant physiology and finds that later depression improvement tracks suppression of each participant's individual alpha frequency rather than generic 10 Hz power change. It is still a small underpowered trial, not a decisive antidepressant efficacy result. But it gives future tACS work a much better target-engagement standard.
