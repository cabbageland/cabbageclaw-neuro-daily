## Short overview

The new preserve is **A whole-brain modeling framework for tDCS montage optimization in drug-resistant epilepsy**.

It is worth keeping because it treats noninvasive stimulation as a patient-specific network-control problem, not as a field-delivery geometry exercise. The authors couple a personalized biophysical head model to a personalized whole-brain seizure-spread model constrained by structural connectivity and SEEG, then use that coupled "neurotwin" to optimize multichannel tDCS montages in 12 drug-resistant epilepsy patients.

The result is useful because it does not ask us to be impressed by optimization-by-construction. The neurotwin loss suppresses simulated spread because that is what it is built to do. The real contribution is the control comparison: field-only montages can deliver similar epileptogenic-zone fields while producing different simulated network outcomes, and the difference often comes down to a few propagation-zone or off-target parcels. In other words, the aggregate field can look fine while the network move is wrong.

## Most relevant paper

**A whole-brain modeling framework for tDCS montage optimization in drug-resistant epilepsy**

This is the most relevant paper today because it is unusually explicit about where field-based stimulation design breaks. Conventional personalized tDCS montage optimization tries to maximize inhibitory field at clinician-selected epileptogenic and propagation regions while sparing the rest. That is reasonable, but incomplete: epilepsy is a spreading network process, so the same field summary can mean different things depending on which parcels are near seizure threshold, which parcels drive other regions, and which off-target parcels get accidentally excited.

The neurotwin pipeline adds the missing layer. It uses MRI-derived finite-element head models to estimate fields, patient-specific structural connectivity to couple neural mass models, and SEEG-derived functional connectivity to personalize seizure-spread dynamics. Candidate montages are then judged partly by whether the simulated network still spreads seizure activity. The search space is not fantasy stimulation: montages are constrained to 10-10 scalp positions, six active electrodes, 2 mA total current, per-electrode current limits, and deliverable electrode configurations.

The strongest result is the dissociation between field similarity and network outcome. Neurotwin and conventional field-based montages had comparable mean fields at epileptogenic-zone parcels, yet the field-based montages produced worse simulated spread outcomes in several patients. Even when a field-only control used a WBM-derived target map, leaving the network term out of the loss could produce lower spread reduction or even increased spread. Minimum-replacement analysis then made the mechanism legible: the important differences often lived in a small number of model propagation-zone parcels that were under-inhibited, propagation-zone parcels that were actively excited, or sensitive non-involved parcels that should have been spared.

The clinical caveat is blunt. The retrospective clinical check is six patients from an open-label tDCS trial. WBM-predicted spread reduction tracked seizure-frequency change in the expected direction, but the association was not significant, and one high-leverage patient had poor model fit. This is a strong method and mechanism paper, not evidence that the proposed montage pipeline is already clinically superior. The right read is sharper: aggregate field optimization is an inadequate proxy for network intervention, and this paper gives a concrete way to make that proxy answer to patient-specific dynamics.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For neuromodulation targeting:** the target is not the coordinate or the field maximum. The target is the controllable role a parcel plays in the patient's dynamics.
- **For tDCS montage optimization:** a good baseline must include matched field-only controls. Otherwise it is too easy to claim a network model helps when only the target map changed.
- **For epilepsy digital twins:** the useful step is moving from diagnostic simulation to therapy design, where the WBM becomes the optimization objective rather than a retrospective explanation.
- **For adaptive and closed-loop stimulation:** today's paper is open-loop montage design, but the principle transfers cleanly: estimate the latent network state you need to control, then test whether the intervention changes that state.
- **For clinical translation:** the current version is SEEG-dependent and only weakly clinically validated. The prospective trial matters, and noninvasive personalization remains the hard next step.
- **For computational baselines:** graph centrality helps but is not enough. High-impact parcels tend to be hubs, yet the remaining variance is exactly why patient-specific dynamics matter.
- **For hypnosis and hypnotherapy:** standing checks surfaced recent hypnosis-neuroimaging material, including perceptual-decoding and MEG event-response work, but nothing today beat the neurotwin paper on intervention-control relevance.
- **For CBT plus interventional psychiatry:** standing checks surfaced combined rTMS/CBT and CBT-after-esketamine items. They remain useful combination-care context, but today's strongest mechanism paper is the epilepsy montage model.

## One-paragraph takeaway

October twelfth's useful lesson is that stimulation design should optimize the network move, not just the field picture. The new neurotwin tDCS paper does not prove clinical superiority, and it should not be oversold. But it makes a clean, transferable point: two montages can look similar by aggregate electric-field metrics while differing at the few parcels that decide whether pathological activity spreads. For cabbageland, that is the steal-worthy frame: put the disease-relevant dynamics inside the intervention objective, then inspect which small changes actually control the system.
