## Short overview

The new preserve is **Dissociable roles of reward prediction error in the contrasting mood dynamics of depression and anxiety**.

This is the right paper to keep today because it takes one compact computational object, reward prediction error sensitivity in momentary mood, and uses it to split depression, anxiety, and shared distress into different affective-updating signatures. Depression-specific symptoms point toward dampened mood fluctuations and lower RPE-driven mood sensitivity. Anxiety-specific symptoms point in the opposite direction in pooled non-clinical data, with higher mood variability and higher RPE sensitivity, though that result is weaker and still needs stronger clinical replication. Shared distress maps more to lower baseline mood and greater risk aversion.

The value is not that a gambling task magically solves mood disorders. The value is the intervention framing: depression and anxiety should not be treated as one generic negative-affect mass when the mechanism of state updating may differ. That matters for CBT, exposure, ketamine, TMS, psychedelic-assisted therapy, and any hybrid intervention that claims to modify learning or affective state transitions.

## Most relevant paper

**Dissociable roles of reward prediction error in the contrasting mood dynamics of depression and anxiety**

This is the most relevant paper today because it gives a clean computational reason not to collapse depression and anxiety into one undifferentiated distress bucket. Across five experiments involving 2043 participants, including a clinical affective-disorders sample, the authors combine bifactor symptom modeling with a trial-level mood model in a gambling task. Momentary mood is modeled as a recency-weighted function of certain rewards, expected values, reward prediction errors, and drift.

The main result is legible. Depression-specific scores are associated with reduced mood variability and reduced RPE-related mood sensitivity, and this depression-linked hyposensitivity replicates in patients. Anxiety-specific scores move in the opposite direction, toward stronger RPE-linked mood swings, but the anxiety result is less robust and depends more on pooled non-clinical power. Shared depression/anxiety burden is not the same thing as either specific factor; it relates more to lower baseline mood and greater risk aversion.

The caveat is important. This is not causal proof, not an intervention trial, and not a clinical decision tool. It is still a stylized task using subjective ratings. But it is a useful marker hypothesis: different affective dimensions may require different learning and state-update readouts if an intervention claims to change mood regulation.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For computational psychiatry:** the paper makes the symptom-decomposition step central instead of optional. RPE mood sensitivity means something different once depression-specific, anxiety-specific, and shared factors are separated.
- **For depression treatment:** affective blunting can be reframed as reduced sensitivity of mood to prediction error, not just low mood or low reward.
- **For anxiety treatment:** the stronger claim is still provisional, but the useful hypothesis is affective hypersensitivity to uncertainty resolution.
- **For intervention design:** CBT, exposure, ketamine, TMS, and psychedelic-assisted therapy can be asked a sharper question: does the treatment alter the mapping from outcome surprise to mood, and for which symptom dimension?
- **For biomarkers:** a task parameter is not automatically a biomarker. The next step is prospective treatment prediction or mediation, not branding beta-RPE as destiny.
- **For hypnosis and hypnotherapy:** the standing check surfaced parasomnia hypnosis and broader clinical hypnosis items, but nothing today beat the computational mood paper on mechanism or transfer value.
- **For CBT plus interventional psychiatry:** the standing check surfaced a preliminary rTMS-plus-CBT gambling-disorder study and ketamine-assisted psychotherapy material. They remain relevant but did not have the mechanistic clarity or evidential strength to displace today's preserve.

## One-paragraph takeaway

October first is a good reminder that comorbidity is not an excuse to stop modeling. If depression and anxiety share distress but differ in how prediction errors move momentary mood, then intervention studies need readouts that can see that difference. The eLife paper does not prove how to treat anyone, but it gives future treatment work a better handle: estimate how a person's mood updates under uncertainty, then test whether a therapy, stimulation protocol, ketamine session, or psychedelic-assisted process changes that update rule in the intended direction.
