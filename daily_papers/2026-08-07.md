## Short overview

The fresh preserve today is the August 4, 2026 arXiv preprint **Modelling temporal dynamics of suicidal ideation and behaviour across pre- to early adolescence using a Markov framework**. It wins because it takes a domain that is usually flattened into generic risk labels and makes it legible as a state-transition problem with age dependence and uncertainty. Using child self-report ABCD data from 11,864 participants across ages 9 to 13, the authors define eight states spanning passive ideation, active ideation, suicidal behaviour, and the presence or absence of co-reported non-suicidal self-injury, or NSSI. They then estimate year-specific transition matrices, multi-year transition probabilities, first-passage probabilities, bootstrap confidence intervals, and entropy-based uncertainty. The result is not magical prediction. It is something better grounded: a structured account of how remission, escalation, persistence, and volatility actually move during this developmental window.

The main signal is sharp enough to matter. Remission to a no-report state is still the most common next-year outcome for most starting states, which is the part a hype machine would use to sound reassuring. But the more useful part is that remission declines with age and with more advanced states, while NSSI consistently pushes trajectories toward higher risk and lower predictability. By age 11-12, active ideation with NSSI carries about a 6 percent next-year probability of suicidal behaviour versus about 4 percent without NSSI. A child with passive ideation at age 9-10 has roughly a 7.8 percent three-year probability of reaching suicidal behaviour, rising to about 11 percent when NSSI is co-reported. That is not just "higher risk." It is a state-dependent change in both direction and uncertainty.

The caveats are real and not hidden. This is annual self-report data, with current and lifetime indicators aggregated in a way that creates temporal ambiguity. The model is first-order Markov, untested for longer-memory effects, and cohort-level rather than individualized prediction. There is no treatment variable, no neurobiology in the state model, and no causal identification. But that does not make it weak. It makes it a useful scaffold. For developmental psychiatry and interventional psychiatry, a model that keeps volatility visible is already a meaningful upgrade over static risk-factor sludge.

## Most relevant paper

**Modelling temporal dynamics of suicidal ideation and behaviour across pre- to early adolescence using a Markov framework**

The useful move here is that the paper refuses two bad defaults at once. First, it does not treat adolescent suicidality as a static trait-like label. Second, it does not force sparse annual data into a fake smooth trajectory story. Instead it represents the problem as movement among eight clinically meaningful states defined by ideation or behaviour severity and whether NSSI is co-reported. That sounds simple, but in this domain simple is often better than fake sophistication.

The method is straightforward and therefore inspectable. The authors use child self-report KSADS-derived ABCD data from 11,864 participants across four annual time points from ages 9-10 through 12-13. They estimate separate year-specific transition matrices, compute multi-year transition probabilities and first-passage probabilities, quantify sampling uncertainty with 100 bootstrap resamples, and quantify dynamical unpredictability with normalized Shannon entropy. The important part is not just what next state is likeliest. It is whether the future distribution is narrow or diffuse, because volatility itself may matter for monitoring and triage.

The results are exactly the kind of pattern interventional and developmental psychiatry should care about. Remission to a no-report state is common, but it becomes less common with age and with more advanced starting states. NSSI is not just a generic bad covariate. It consistently lowers remission, raises escalation to or persistence in suicidal behaviour, and increases entropy. By age 11-12, active ideation with NSSI carries about a 6 percent next-year transition probability into suicidal behaviour, compared with about 4 percent without NSSI. For passive ideation at age 9-10, the three-year probability of reaching suicidal behaviour rises from about 7.8 percent to about 11 percent when NSSI is co-reported. Child-parent disagreement is also huge, with most reports endorsed by only one informant, which is its own practical warning about whose observations a monitoring system is really built on.

The paper does not solve the hard problem of individualized prediction or mechanism. The Markov property is assumed rather than proven. Annual self-report compresses within-year onset and remission. Current and lifetime categories are aggregated. The cohort has the usual ABCD representativeness and attrition concerns. There is no treatment history or biomarker inside the state model. But as a framing and modeling paper, it is unusually useful because it operationalizes what is often said too loosely: that suicidal trajectories are unstable, developmentally shifting, and not fully captured by average risk. The best next step is obvious. Put intervention variables and biology into this state logic instead of discarding the state logic.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For developmental suicidality:** state-transition and uncertainty models are a better default than ever-versus-never labels or forced smooth latent curves when data are sparse and volatility is real.
- **For interventional psychiatry:** NSSI looks less like a background severity covariate and more like a modifier of both escalation risk and trajectory unpredictability.
- **For monitoring design:** uncertainty itself may be a usable triage signal; a nominally "improved" child can still sit on a high-entropy trajectory that deserves follow-up.
- **For modeling taste:** you do not need a decorative latent-state monster when an explicit time-varying state model already exposes the clinically important structure.
- **For standing-interest combo papers:** the July 2026 rTMS-plus-CBT review looks like implementation synthesis rather than a new mechanistic step, which is useful context but not today's best preserve.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 7 lesson is that volatility is not just noise around risk. Today's preserve matters because it shows that NSSI changes not only the average direction of early-adolescent suicidal trajectories but also how predictable those trajectories are. That does not make a first-order Markov chain the final answer. It does make static risk framing look too blunt for the problem.
