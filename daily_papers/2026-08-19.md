## Short overview

The fresh preserve today is the August 18, 2026 arXiv paper **Continual-learning rules shape representational drift**. It wins because it does something more useful than saying representational drift exists and then vaguely gesturing at plasticity. The paper turns drift into a concrete diagnostic of the stability-plasticity trade-off. That is the right move for anyone trying to reason about longitudinal biomarkers, adaptive decoders, or closed-loop systems that silently assume the brain should keep using a fixed internal code forever.

The setup is simple enough to stay legible and strong enough to matter. The authors compare continual-learning rules in two regimes: a ResNet-18 trained on 20 sequential five-class ImageNet tasks, and a continuous-time recurrent network trained on 18 sequential cognitive tasks from the Yang task family. They then keep probing the same task-1 inputs across later checkpoints to ask three questions. Does the method retain old performance? Does the internal representation drift anyway? And if it drifts, what part of the geometry is actually moving?

The useful answer is clean. Experience replay preserves old-task performance best, but the representation still drifts progressively as more tasks intervene. Later visual layers drift more than earlier ones, and in the recurrent network the temporal tuning of units drifts more than their mean activity. So the code is not fixed, but it is not random mush either. Coarser class structure and trial-epoch organization survive even while unit-level or checkpoint-level similarity decays.

The strongest result is the intervention rather than the observation. Inside the replay regime, the authors explicitly anchor old representations and show that stronger anchoring suppresses drift while also reducing forward learning on new tasks. That matters because it pushes drift away from the usual nuisance-noise framing. Under these conditions, drift looks like a cost of staying plastic, not merely a failure to stay still.

This is not a biological proof. The paper uses machine-learning task sequences, not longitudinal neural recordings, and its replay mechanism is much cleaner than real hippocampal or cortical rehearsal. But that is exactly why it is useful. It isolates a mechanistic proposition that can travel: if a learning system must retain behavior while continuing to adapt, then the internal code may need room to move. That is a sharper starting point than pretending stability means every useful representation should stay frozen.

## Most relevant paper

**Continual-learning rules shape representational drift**

The useful move here is refusing to treat drift as either pure nuisance or mystical biological texture. The paper asks a sharper question: if a learning system has to retain old behavior while continuing to learn, what kind of representational motion naturally appears under different anti-forgetting rules? That reframes drift from a descriptive phenomenon into a mechanistic consequence of how the system buys stability.

The design is strong because it spans both static and dynamic representations. In the ImageNet track, the authors compare naive sequential training, elastic weight consolidation, learning without forgetting, and experience replay in a ResNet-18 across 20 sequential five-class tasks. In the cognitive track, they train a continuous-time recurrent network on 18 sequential cognitive tasks and measure drift in population vectors, whole-trial trajectories, mean unit activity, and temporal tuning. Replay preserves old performance best, but the code still moves. Later CNN layers are more labile than earlier ones, and in the recurrent network temporal tuning drifts more than mean activity. That is the key pattern: the fine code moves more than the coarse task geometry.

The decisive result is the anchoring experiment. When the authors add an explicit penalty that keeps old replay representations close to their task-1 form, drift goes down but forward learning gets worse. That does not prove all biological drift is good. It does show, under these conditions, that forcing the code to stay still can directly tax future plasticity. For cabbageland, that is a useful warning label on any longitudinal decoder, biomarker, or control scheme that assumes internal representations should remain geometrically fixed over time.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For drift framing:** the paper argues that representational drift can be a signature of the rule used to protect old knowledge, not just measurement noise or neural sloppiness.
- **For longitudinal biomarkers:** stable behavior or retained task performance does not guarantee a fixed unit-level code, so coarse geometry may be a better anchor than individual features.
- **For continual-learning evaluation:** accuracy alone hides whether a method preserves function by replay-driven reorganization or by effectively freezing the system.
- **For adaptive control:** closed-loop systems may need features and state estimators that tolerate internal representational motion rather than assuming a stationary codebook.
- **For archive logic:** the paper slots naturally between the whole-brain state-transition note and the TMS field-to-state coupling note by clarifying what kinds of internal movement may be compatible with retained function.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 19 lesson is that stable outputs do not imply fixed internal codes. Today's preserve matters because it shows, in a fully readable computational study across both feedforward and recurrent settings, that replay can preserve old behavior while representations drift in structured ways, and that directly suppressing that drift hurts later learning. It is not a neural-data paper, so it does not settle biological drift. But it gives a much sharper mechanistic default: some representational motion may be the cost of staying plastic while keeping behavior intact.
