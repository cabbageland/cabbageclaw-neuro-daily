## Short overview

The fresh preserve today is the August 15, 2026 arXiv paper **Phase- and amplitude-dependent control of synchronization in excitatory-inhibitory networks via pulsed stimulation**. It wins because it does something a lot of phase-locked neuromodulation papers still dodge. It asks not just whether a pulse shifts network phase, but whether it actually weakens oscillatory activity and synchrony, and whether that useful window survives repeated stimulation. The paper is a fully readable mechanistic control note, not an abstract-only gamble, and that matters because the useful claim lives in the results section rather than in decorative framing.

The core setup is intentionally simple and therefore legible. The authors simulate a balanced 1,000-neuron excitatory-inhibitory exponential integrate-and-fire network with sparse connectivity and coherent oscillations around **27.3 Hz**. They then sweep brief current pulses across the oscillation cycle and jointly analyze three outputs: the network phase response curve, the network amplitude response curve, and synchrony change through the population Fano factor. That is the right move. A phase shift alone does not tell you whether you actually disrupted a pathological rhythm or merely nudged its timing.

The useful result is clean. The same pulse can synchronize, do almost nothing, or desynchronize the network depending only on stimulation phase. For a representative **225 pA** pulse, **phi = 0.90** is synchronizing, **phi = 0.70** is basically ineffective, and **phi = 0.50** is desynchronizing. The strongest negative amplitude responses and the strongest synchrony reductions cluster near **phi approximately 0.5**, and when the authors repeat stimulation for ten consecutive cycles at that phase, the network progressively weakens and desynchronizes without shifting the optimal phase itself. The robustness story is decent for a model paper too: the cumulative effect survives pulse amplitudes from **50 to 250 pA**, inhibitory synaptic decay constants from **5 to 7 ms**, and five independent network realizations.

That does not make it translationally solved. The network is still homogeneous, randomly connected, and assigned fixed delays. There is no measurement noise, no phase-estimation failure, no stimulation artifact problem, no patient heterogeneity, and no symptom-linked objective. But that is exactly why the paper is useful as a control baseline rather than a clinical promise. It says future phase-targeted DBS, TMS, tACS, or ultrasound papers should not get away with showing only phase-reset curves and calling it control.

## Most relevant paper

**Phase- and amplitude-dependent control of synchronization in excitatory-inhibitory networks via pulsed stimulation**

The strategic value here is not merely that phase matters. Plenty of papers say that and then show one attractive response curve. The useful move is insisting that phase response is not enough. The paper makes amplitude suppression and synchrony reduction first-class outputs, which is much closer to what an intervention actually cares about when the target phenomenon is pathological oscillation.

The setup is clean enough to be reusable. The authors simulate a balanced 1,000-neuron excitatory-inhibitory network, characterize its baseline oscillation around 27.3 Hz, and then sweep brief pulses across the cycle while measuring network phase response, network amplitude response, and synchrony change. That three-axis map immediately reveals that the same stimulation can do opposite things depending on timing. For a representative 225 pA pulse, phase 0.90 is synchronizing, phase 0.70 is nearly inert, and phase 0.50 is desynchronizing. Repeated pulses near the desynchronizing phase progressively weaken oscillatory activity and synchrony, and the best phase window stays put.

The important caveat is that this is still a toy-model control paper. The network is homogeneous, randomly connected, and built with fixed delays. There is no phase-estimation noise, no artifact problem, no plasticity, and no clinical endpoint. So the preserve is not "this solves real-world closed-loop stimulation." The preserve is "future phase-targeted neuromodulation papers should probably have to show amplitude and synchrony control, not just phase resetting."

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For phase-targeted control framing:** the paper argues that nPRC alone is an incomplete control object.
- **For evaluation practice:** future stimulation papers should probably report amplitude and synchrony response maps alongside phase-reset curves.
- **For repeated stimulation logic:** it shows that a stable desynchronizing window can remain useful even as repeated pulses move the network into a weaker-synchrony operating point.
- **For model baselines:** a clean balanced EI-network benchmark with explicit nPRC, nARC, and pFF analyses is a better baseline than generic one-curve phase-reset demonstrations.
- **For archive logic:** it slots naturally between the human dynamic-FC phase note and the minimax neurostimulation note by giving a concrete mechanistic middle layer.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 18 lesson is that phase-targeted neuromodulation needs a richer control vocabulary than phase alone. Today's preserve matters because it shows, in a fully readable mechanistic model, that the same pulse can synchronize, fail, or desynchronize an oscillatory network depending only on timing, and that the desynchronizing window near phase 0.5 stays useful under repetition. It is still a toy-model paper, not a translational proof. But it is the right kind of paper for sharpening what real closed-loop claims should have to demonstrate.
