## Short overview

The best new preserve today is **Connectome gradient dysfunction in earthquake-induced posttraumatic stress disorder and its association with gene expression and neurotransmitter profiles**. It is a full-text Psychological Medicine paper, and it earns the note because it gives PTSD a sharper network object than another collection of abnormal regions: the principal unimodal-to-transmodal connectome gradient.

In 86 untreated adult earthquake-related PTSD patients and 86 trauma-exposed non-PTSD controls, the authors estimate voxelwise resting-state functional-connectome gradients using diffusion map embedding. PTSD shows a more extended and variable principal gradient, with higher scores in visual and limbic systems and lower scores in ventral attention and frontoparietal systems. CAPS severity correlates with visual, limbic, and ventral-attention gradient scores across the combined trauma-exposed sample, though not within the PTSD group alone. A smaller adolescent comparison suggests stronger PTSD-related subcortical gradient reduction in adolescents than adults. The gradient-difference map also colocalizes with gene-expression patterns enriched for mitochondrial and DNA/RNA metabolic processes and with dopamine, opioid, noradrenaline, and cholinergic neurotransmitter maps.

The transferable idea is useful: persistent PTSD may involve a distorted sensory-to-transmodal hierarchy, not just a hot amygdala or weak prefrontal control slogan. That pairs naturally with the archive note **A visual cortex origin of trauma memory intrusions**, which made intrusive trauma imagery a visual-cortical access problem, and with **Personalized fMRI-Guided TMS Targeting the Threat Neurocircuitry in PTSD**, which asked whether individualized threat-circuit stimulation can change trauma-relevant reactivity. It also belongs near **Fear Learning-Induced Brain Dynamics Predict Individual Extinction Memory Expression following Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation**, because that note framed stimulation effects through the state produced by learning.

## Most relevant paper

**Connectome gradient dysfunction in earthquake-induced posttraumatic stress disorder and its association with gene expression and neurotransmitter profiles**

This is the most relevant paper today because it turns a common PTSD neuroimaging mess into a cleaner geometry question. The usual story says PTSD involves visual processing, threat memory, limbic response, salience, and top-down control. True enough, but too loose. This paper asks whether those systems are displaced along the brain's principal functional hierarchy from unimodal sensory cortex to transmodal association cortex.

The method is serious enough to use and limited enough to critique. The authors build voxelwise resting-state functional connectomes, keep strong connections, compute profile similarity, estimate the principal gradient by diffusion map embedding, and align gradients across participants. They then compare global, subnetwork, and voxelwise gradient measures between PTSD and trauma-exposed controls while controlling age, sex, education, time since trauma, and motion.

The result is a hierarchy-distortion account. PTSD has a wider, more variable principal gradient. Visual and limbic networks shift higher; ventral attention and frontoparietal networks shift lower. Symptom severity tracks those network measures across the whole trauma-exposed sample, but the lack of within-PTSD correlations means the map may distinguish PTSD from resilient trauma exposure better than it grades severity among already-affected patients. That distinction matters.

The molecular layer is useful but should stay in its lane. The gradient-difference map aligns with healthy-donor gene-expression patterns enriched for mitochondria and DNA/RNA metabolism and with dopamine D2, kappa opioid, noradrenaline transporter, and cholinergic VAChT distributions. That is hypothesis generation, not mechanism proof. Still, it is a better bridge from network geometry to biology than another decorative neurotransmitter paragraph.

The caveats are real: cross-sectional data, no direct cognitive testing, healthy-donor transcriptomic atlases, no nontraumatized control group, and one disaster-specific comorbidity-free cohort. But the paper gives future trauma intervention work a useful target: not just lowering threat reactivity, but changing the hierarchy that determines how sensory memory, salience, attention, and control are separated or entangled.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For PTSD network neuroscience:** the paper makes sensory-to-transmodal hierarchy a central object, not a background visualization.
- **For trauma-memory framing:** visual and limbic gradient elevation pairs well with trauma-intrusion work; intrusive imagery may be easier to discuss as altered sensory-memory access within a warped hierarchy.
- **For intervention psychiatry:** threat-circuit TMS, exposure, MDMA-assisted therapy, and other trauma treatments should ask whether they normalize or reorganize hierarchy, not only whether symptoms move.
- **For developmental psychiatry:** the adolescent subcortical gradient interaction is preliminary, but it points toward trauma timing as a hierarchy-development problem.
- **For biomarkers:** a group-separating gradient map is not automatically a dimensional severity marker; within-PTSD validation matters.
- **For molecular interpretation:** atlas colocalization should generate hypotheses about dopamine, opioid, noradrenaline, cholinergic, mitochondrial, and metabolic involvement; it should not be sold as direct molecular measurement.
- **For CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry:** the tDCS-plus-CBI meta-analysis is useful because it blocks weak combination claims with negative sham-controlled evidence.
- **For neuroengineering:** spatially selective VNS remains interesting, but today's IOP access path was challenge-gated, so it needs a future full-text route before preservation.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The September fifteenth lesson is that PTSD may be better framed as distorted hierarchy than as a bag of abnormal regions. Today's preserve does not prove causality, treatment prediction, or a ready biomarker. It does something more useful upstream: it says persistent post-trauma symptoms may reflect altered separation between sensory/limbic systems and attention/control systems, and it shows how that hierarchy can be tested against development and molecular atlases. For cabbageland, that is a clean bridge between trauma imagery, fear learning, hypnosis/state change, and neuromodulation.
