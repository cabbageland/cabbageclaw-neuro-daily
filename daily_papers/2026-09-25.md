## Short overview

The new preserve is **Low-Intensity Focused Ultrasound of the Amygdala in Depression and Anxiety: A First-in-Human Active-Controlled Trial**.

It is worth keeping because it does the right first thing for deep noninvasive psychiatry: before claiming treatment efficacy, it asks whether low-intensity focused ultrasound can safely and selectively engage a deep affective target in patients. The answer is cautiously useful. Right-amygdala FUS produced multimodal target-engagement signals relative to active S1 control, including ASL perfusion, BOLD activity, and resting-state connectivity changes. But symptoms did not clearly separate by condition, adverse events were more frequent after amygdala sonication, and one participant worsened after a very large perfusion shift.

The digest point is simple: target engagement is necessary, not sufficient. In focused ultrasound psychiatry, the next useful question is not whether a small preprint can be spun into efficacy. It is how to define a safe response window, verify where acoustic energy actually lands, and link acute circuit perturbation to durable clinical change.

## Most relevant paper

**Low-Intensity Focused Ultrasound of the Amygdala in Depression and Anxiety: A First-in-Human Active-Controlled Trial**

This is the most relevant paper today because it is a clean example of precision-first translational neuromodulation. Ten veterans with MDD received right-amygdala FUS and left-S1 active-control FUS in a randomized crossover design. Amygdala sonication produced greater target perfusion change than S1 control or adjacent hippocampal head, engaged vmPFC/rACC/insula during sonication, and decreased basolateral-amygdala connectivity with sensorimotor regions after sonication.

The important restraint is just as valuable as the positive signal. The study was not powered for efficacy, symptom improvement did not separate cleanly by condition, and one participant had protocol-defined worsening after a 441 percent target-region perfusion increase. That makes the paper a useful argument for dose-response work, not a victory lap.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For clinical/translational neuromodulation:** today's preserve strengthens the case that FUS can engage deep affective targets in patients, but it also makes safety and dose-window modeling impossible to ignore.
- **For focused ultrasound baselines:** the active-control amygdala result should be read alongside the earlier amygdala tFUS target-engagement/treatment paper and the subcallosal-cingulate depression pilot. The design standard is converging: show target engagement, then test clinical durability separately.
- **For intervention logic:** the strongest design lesson is endpoint separation. Perfusion, BOLD, resting-state connectivity, subjective state reports, and symptom scores are different proof surfaces.
- **For state estimation / biomarkers:** ASL perfusion may be a useful target-engagement marker, but the worsening case warns against treating bigger physiological movement as monotonically better.

## One-paragraph takeaway

September twenty-fifth is a useful reminder that deep-target psychiatry should earn every rung of its ladder. The new amygdala FUS trial is exciting because it shows anatomically specific physiological engagement in patients against an active-control target. It is also sobering because clinical improvement did not separate cleanly, adverse events were more common after amygdala sonication, and one participant worsened after an outsized perfusion response. The steal-worthy principle is not "ultrasound works for depression." It is "precision, safety, dose window, and target engagement come before efficacy poetry."
