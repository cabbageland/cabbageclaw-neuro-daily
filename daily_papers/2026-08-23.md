## Short overview

The new preserve today is the Journal of Neural Engineering paper **Dose-dependent power and connectivity modulation of low frequency oscillations through transcranial magnetic stimulation in non-human primates**. It wins because it is one of the cleaner recent target-engagement papers in the left-prefrontal TMS lane. Instead of asking whether a protocol name sounds plausible, it asks what single pulses over left prefrontal cortex actually do to local and distributed low-frequency dynamics when you can read the network directly with intracranial electrodes.

The design is small, but much less decorative than the average mechanism claim. Two rhesus macaques with implanted stereo-EEG depth electrodes received single biphasic left-prefrontal TMS pulses at six intensities from 10 percent to 125 percent of maximum stimulator output, plus a passive click-only control. The key result is a three-stage response in 1 to 13 hertz activity: an early low-frequency power increase near the stimulation site, a broader later suppression across frontal, temporal, and parietal contacts, and a partial rebound at a subset of contacts. The important extra layer is that early power enhancement and early phase-based connectivity enhancement scale with intensity, while later suppression and rebound look more thresholded and less cleanly dose-tuned.

That is exactly the kind of distinction the human clinical literature often blurs. The field talks as if "stronger stimulation" simply means stronger engagement, when the more honest story may be stronger early excitation paired with stronger later suppression. This paper does not solve the clinical translation problem, but it gives a more believable mechanistic template for what left-prefrontal TMS may actually do to a connected network.

The limits are real. The work is in two anesthetized monkeys, one animal alone received the highest 125 percent condition, and some delayed low-frequency effects may be contaminated by auditory responses to the TMS click. But those caveats do not cancel the main value. They just keep the result in the right bucket: translational target-engagement physiology, not treatment proof.

The fresh comparison lane had one clinically interesting hybrid paper and two weaker mechanistic lookalikes. The full-text PMC paper **A randomized double-blind sham-controlled preliminary study of a 5-week protocol of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) combined with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for gambling disorder in Indonesia** is worth noting because it is an explicit CBT-plus-intervention psychiatry test. But it randomized only 27 participants, both groups improved on overall gambling symptom scores, and the active arm mainly looked faster on craving and some cognition-linked subscales rather than cleaner on the main syndrome outcome. Interesting combination logic signal, still not preserve-grade.

The PubMed abstract **Synergistic entrainment: a multiscale neurodynamic framework for closed-loop cross-modal neuromodulation** sounds as if it should be a perfect cabbageland paper. In practice it still reads like conceptual packaging. The framing is about rhythmic multimodal inputs, phase alignment, and macro-scale synchronization, but there is no empirical control result, no real closed-loop benchmark, and no evidence yet that the framework earns more than a hypothesis badge. The adolescent depression item **Feasibility, safety, and preliminary clinical efficacy of resting-state functional connectivity MRI-guided rTMS for adolescents with major depressive disorder: Protocol for a randomized, double-blind, three-arm, sham-controlled pilot trial** is directionally relevant but still only a protocol, not outcome data.

The archive anchors help keep the day balanced instead of turning it into one more "left DLPFC good" bulletin. **Precision phase targeting of event-related oscillations using real-time closed-loop TMS-EEG** remains the cleaner control note on when timing becomes the real variable. **Transcranial magnetic stimulation of visual-motion area V5/MT modulates sensory thalamus responses during visual speech recognition** remains the sharper human causal-perturbation note on distributed corticothalamic consequences. **A Whole-Brain Dynamical Framework Linking Resting-State Activity to TMS-Evoked Responses** still does better than most papers at connecting perturbation spread to intrinsic dynamics. **Effective correction of extreme capacitive artifacts in TMS-EEG via windowed detrending** still matters because mechanistic talk collapses fast if the sensing stack is mostly artifact recovery.

The standing-interest checks did not beat the archive. In the **hypnosis and hypnotherapy** lane, the visible 2026 paper **The efficacy of clinical hypnosis in treating NREM and REM parasomnias: A retrospective study** is a reasonable sleep-clinic outcomes paper, but it is retrospective, mechanism-thin, and mostly useful for symptom-response rates rather than for state-change logic or biomarkers. It does not beat the existing hypnosis anchor [Decoding hypnotic consciousness: neural and experiential insights into induced and ideomotor suggestions](https://cabbageland.github.io/cabbageclaw-neuro-daily-web/paper_notes/decoding_hypnotic_consciousness_ideomotor). In the **CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry** lane, the gambling-disorder rTMS-plus-CBT paper is real enough to watch, but it still does not beat the more generally useful implementation anchor [Noninvasive brain stimulation combined with evidence-based psychotherapy for psychiatric disorders: A meta-analysis of optimal implementation parameters](https://cabbageland.github.io/cabbageclaw-neuro-daily-web/paper_notes/nibs_psychotherapy_meta_analysis).

So the useful August 23 lesson is not "prefrontal TMS changes the brain." Of course it does. The sharper lesson is that intensity-sensitive engagement may be temporally structured: stronger pulses can buy more early low-frequency propagation while also buying more prolonged later suppression. If future closed-loop or personalized TMS work wants mechanistic credibility, it should stop pretending those phases are the same thing.

## Most relevant paper

**Dose-dependent power and connectivity modulation of low frequency oscillations through transcranial magnetic stimulation in non-human primates**

The paper matters because it is asking a more adult question than most prefrontal TMS papers ask. Instead of hoping that left-prefrontal stimulation somehow improves symptoms and then reverse-engineering a mechanism from scalp noise, it directly measures how stimulation intensity changes oscillatory power and phase-based coupling in an intracranially sampled network.

The useful result is not just "higher intensity does more." It is more structured than that. The paper shows an early low-frequency power increase near the stimulation site, then a broader low-frequency suppression across connected frontal, temporal, and parietal regions, and then a rebound in a subset of contacts around 1500 milliseconds. The early enhancement in both power and connectivity scales with intensity. The later suppression is stronger at higher intensities too, but it looks less like clean scaling and more like crossing into a longer suppressive regime.

That distinction is what makes the paper worth preserving. It says stronger left-prefrontal TMS may not simply mean more target engagement in one direction. It may mean a temporally structured tradeoff: more immediate propagation and more delayed suppression. That is the kind of detail future adaptive or state-sensitive stimulation work should care about if it wants to do more than rename fixed-parameter dosing.

The caveats are obvious and still important. The data come from two anesthetized rhesus macaques, not awake clinical populations. The highest 125 percent condition appears in one monkey only. Some delayed effects may reflect auditory confounding. And the full text available in this environment was the PMC-hosted bioRxiv preprint rather than line-by-line journal HTML. But even with those limits, the paper gives a much sharper target-engagement picture than the average human prefrontal TMS paper.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For target-engagement logic:** stronger prefrontal TMS pulses should not be summarized as a single monotonic "more engagement" variable; early enhancement and later suppression need to be tracked separately.
- **For translational physiology:** intracranial LFP and connectivity readouts in a primate model can expose network consequences that scalp EEG often smears or hides.
- **For closed-loop design:** if early low-frequency enhancement is the useful window, adaptive protocols should try to exploit that phase without paying unnecessary cost in prolonged suppression.
- **For anatomical interpretation:** distal effects in ventral insula, posterior cingulate, parietal, and parahippocampal regions make structural-pathway propagation a better story than local-only target mythology.
- **For current comparison material:** the full-text CBT-plus-rTMS gambling paper, the adolescent fcMRI-guided rTMS protocol, and the synergistic-entrainment framework all matter directionally, but none beats a direct intracranial target-engagement paper today.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 23 lesson is that mechanistic credibility in prefrontal TMS should come from tracing what the stimulation actually does to a network across time, not from repeating the protocol label more loudly. Today's preserve matters because it shows with intracranial recordings that stronger pulses buy a bigger early low-frequency power and connectivity boost but also a bigger and longer later suppressive phase. That is a much better control-oriented framing than the usual "left DLPFC stimulation engages the target" slogan. It is still translational physiology rather than clinical proof, but it gives future adaptive TMS work a stricter target-engagement template.
