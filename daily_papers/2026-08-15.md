## Short overview

The fresh preserve today is the August 15, 2026 arXiv paper **Activity-dependent epidemic spreading on multiscale brain networks predicts Alzheimer's disease progression**. It wins because it does something more useful than another anatomy-only diffusion story. The paper explicitly asks how neuronal activity reshapes *whether* pathology can spread and *where* it goes first, then connects that theory to longitudinal human tau-PET rather than leaving it as decorative spectral math.

The core move is to couple a node-activity process to susceptible-infected-susceptible spreading on a structural brain network. That gives the authors two intervention-adjacent objects: an **activity-dependent epidemic threshold**, which says how easy it is for a small pathological seed to take off, and an **activity-dependent dominant eigenmode**, which says where growth should concentrate first. They then show that these activity-weighted corrections are associated with later regional tau accumulation in human imaging cohorts beyond anatomy-only structure, plain FDG averages, or generic amyloid nuisance adjustment. The strongest regional results are not tiny: first-order and second-order correction terms land around partial Spearman correlations of **0.441**, **-0.428**, **0.386**, and **0.436**, while fitted activity-dependent dominant modes reach partial correlations of **0.452** to **0.471**. The individual-level threshold result is more modest, with the best signal at low-to-moderate amyloid burden where inverse threshold reaches **ρ = 0.219** in the **centiloid < 50** subgroup.

That does not make it a ready-made clinical model. The framework is still an early-invasion model, not a full disease-course simulator. The structural network is group-averaged rather than patient-specific. Regional glucose metabolism is only a proxy for neuronal activity, and the nicest threshold result depends on the observed best centiloid cutoff, so those p-values are nominal. Still, it is worth preserving because it upgrades disease-spread reasoning from “tau follows structure” to “tau follows structure *as modulated by activity and hidden multiscale variation*,” which is a more serious control and targeting vocabulary.

## Most relevant paper

**Activity-dependent epidemic spreading on multiscale brain networks predicts Alzheimer's disease progression**

The strategic value here is not that the paper says pathology spreads on networks. Everyone in this lane says that. The useful move is that it makes **neuronal activity** part of the spreading operator rather than a post hoc biological garnish, and then asks how that changes both the epidemic threshold and the dominant invasion mode. That is a more interesting framing because it turns disease progression into a state-dependent network problem rather than a static anatomy problem.

The human-data section is what makes it worth keeping. The authors use a group structural connectome, normative FDG-PET means and variances, and longitudinal tau-PET from ADNI, HABS, and A4 to test whether activity-weighted spectral quantities predict where tau grows next. The answer is directionally yes. Activity-dependent correction terms and fitted dominant modes stay associated with future regional tau accumulation after adjustment for structural mode, PET inputs, and amyloid. The threshold analysis is weaker but still suggestive in the early-to-middle amyloid range, which is exactly where a transition-style framing should matter most.

What it still does not solve matters just as much. This is not patient-specific targeting. FDG is only a crude proxy for activity, within-region variability is inferred rather than directly measured, and the threshold result is modest and cutoff-sensitive. So this is not a personalized intervention engine. It is a cleaner conceptual scaffold for how activity and connectome structure may jointly shape runaway pathology.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For disease-spread framing:** the paper argues that anatomy-only propagation models are underspecified when neuronal activity changes transmission itself.
- **For spectral language:** it makes the epidemic threshold and dominant eigenmode usable biological objects rather than pure graph-theory decoration.
- **For multiscale modeling:** it shows that unresolved within-region activity variation can still matter and can be approximated from region-level summaries.
- **For neurodegeneration baselines:** future tau-spread papers should probably report whether activity-weighted corrections outperform plain structural modes and PET marginals.
- **For intervention logic:** if activity alters both spread onset and direction, then activity-modulating interventions should be evaluated as network-state perturbations, not only as region-wise treatments.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 15 lesson is that structure alone is probably too thin a story for pathological spread. Today's preserve matters because it couples neuronal activity to network epidemic dynamics, then shows that the resulting threshold and eigenmode language predicts longitudinal human tau progression better than anatomy-only structure does. It is still a group-level early-disease model, not a patient-specific control system. But it gives a sharper vocabulary for talking about when pathology takes off and where it goes next.
