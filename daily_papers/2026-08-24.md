## Short overview

The best fresh preserve today is the arXiv full-text paper **Phase- and amplitude-dependent control of synchronization in excitatory-inhibitory networks via pulsed stimulation**. It wins because it does one thing the phase-targeting literature often gestures at but does not cash out cleanly: it treats phase reset, oscillation amplitude, and synchrony as different response dimensions instead of pretending that a phase response curve alone tells you whether stimulation actually helps.

The paper studies a balanced excitatory-inhibitory network of 1,000 exponential integrate-and-fire neurons with sparse random connectivity and baseline collective oscillations around 27.3 hertz. Brief two-millisecond current pulses are delivered at different oscillation phases and intensities. The useful result is not merely that timing matters. It is that the same pulse can synchronize, do almost nothing, or desynchronize the network depending on when it lands, and the cleanest desynchronizing window is not identified by phase shift alone. It emerges when the network amplitude response curve and the synchrony metric both go negative, centered around a half-cycle phase window near phi equals 0.5.

That is the right level of mechanistic severity for control-oriented stimulation work. The paper shows that a perturbation can advance or delay the rhythm without necessarily weakening the oscillation, and conversely that the most useful stimulation phase is the one that suppresses amplitude and collective synchrony rather than merely resetting time. Repeated pulses at the desynchronizing phase progressively reduce synchrony across pulse intensities from 50 to 250 picoamps, across inhibitory synaptic time constants from 5 to 7 milliseconds, and across five independent network realizations, while the optimal phase window itself stays largely stable. That is a much more actionable control story than generic “phase-specific stimulation matters.”

The limits are also obvious. This is still a computational paper with a simplified random excitatory-inhibitory architecture, full-population stimulation, no empirical neural recordings, and no realistic sensing constraint. The synchrony readout is population Fano factor rather than a clinical biomarker. So this is not a deployable deep brain stimulation policy. But it is still a better conceptual building block for closed-loop neuromodulation than most fresh clinical abstracts today because it separates the control dimensions cleanly and shows which one actually tracks desynchronization.

## Most relevant paper

**Phase- and amplitude-dependent control of synchronization in excitatory-inhibitory networks via pulsed stimulation** is the most relevant paper today because it tightens a core control mistake in the stimulation literature. It argues, with full-text mechanistic detail, that timing a pulse to an oscillation is not enough if you do not also ask whether the pulse actually suppresses amplitude and collective synchrony. The robust half-cycle desynchronizing window near phi equals 0.5, preserved across repeated pulses and parameter sweeps, is exactly the kind of control object that future closed-loop stimulation papers should have to specify.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For phase-targeting logic:** phase response alone is too weak a control summary; amplitude response and synchrony change need to be tracked separately.
- **For adaptive stimulation framing:** a fixed optimal phase can remain useful even as repeated pulses move the network into a less synchronized operating point.
- **For mechanistic benchmarks:** fresh abstract-only target-engagement papers in NAc DBS and DLPFC-to-SGC TMS are interesting, but today they do not beat a full-text paper that states its control geometry more explicitly.
- **For theoretical comparison:** the adjacent event-triggered desynchronization paper is mathematically useful, but the more neuro-grounded preserve wins because it stays closer to stimulation response geometry in a neuronal network rather than only proving control on a generic oscillator class.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 24 lesson is that phase-targeted stimulation should not be scored by phase alignment alone. The preserve today matters because it shows that the same pulse can retime a rhythm, suppress its amplitude, or reduce its synchrony, and those are not interchangeable outcomes. The clean desynchronizing window sits where amplitude response and synchrony both move in the right direction, and that window stays fairly stable even as repeated pulses weaken the network. That is a much sharper control template than the usual “stimulate at the right phase” slogan.
