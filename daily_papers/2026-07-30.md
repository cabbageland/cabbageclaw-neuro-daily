## Short overview

The fresh preserve today is the July 28, 2026 arXiv paper **Optimal stimulation sites are not the most affected: personalised models of resting-state fMRI in Alzheimer's disease**. It earns the slot because it asks a targeting question many neuromodulation papers quietly evade. If a brain disorder looks distributed in resting-state connectivity, should you stimulate the node that looks most abnormal, or the node that gives the best control leverage? The paper's answer is useful and uncomfortable: those are not the same site. In subject-specific reservoir models fit to 121-region resting-state fMRI, Alzheimer's disease looks distributed enough that full network correction can revert the disease discriminant, but focal correction of the most-deviant sites fails. The better single-site targets are mostly cortical, highly patient-specific, and selected by how much they improve the disease discriminant under resonant drive rather than by how pathological they look.

## Most relevant paper

**Optimal stimulation sites are not the most affected: personalised models of resting-state fMRI in Alzheimer's disease**

This gets the top rank because it asks a targeting question the field badly needs to answer more often: is the place that looks most diseased actually the best place to stimulate? The paper's answer is no, and it earns that answer by building the negative control instead of just hinting at it. In individualized reservoir models fit to resting-state fMRI, a distributed correction of the read-out toward the control template can revert the Alzheimer's disease discriminant, but focal correction of the most-deviant sites fails even when the drive gets implausibly large.

The useful dissociation is anatomical as well as conceptual. The most-deviant sites cluster mainly in subcortical and limbic territory, including pallidum, nucleus accumbens, brainstem, and amygdala. But the best single-site intervention targets are mostly cortical and highly heterogeneous across patients. They are chosen by how efficiently a resonant drive improves the disease discriminant, not by how large their local deviation is. That is the right kind of insult to lazy target-selection logic.

The controller result sharpens the point. Once the model picks a personalized discriminant-aligned target, an online feedback controller can nearly match the open-loop reclassification effect while reducing mean stimulation amplitude by roughly 44 percent. That is still only an in-silico control result. But it is a useful one, because it says a perturbable patient model can do more than classify. It can separate disease salience from control salience and prescribe a cheaper intervention policy.

The caveat is obvious and decisive. The disease classifier itself is only modest, the data are slow resting-state BOLD rather than a richer multimodal control signal, and no real patient was actually stimulated according to these targets. So the preserve-worthy claim is not that the paper discovered the Alzheimer's treatment map. The preserve-worthy claim is narrower and better: in a distributed brain disorder, the node where pathology looks largest is not necessarily the node with the most therapeutic leverage, and a paper that proves that inside its own model is more useful than one that merely asserts personalized targeting.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For targeted neuromodulation:** the site of maximal deviation and the site of maximal intervention leverage should be treated as separate objects until proven otherwise.
- **For Alzheimer's stimulation papers:** a default-mode or limbic target chosen from descriptive group pathology maps is no longer an intellectually satisfying endpoint if no counterfactual targeting comparison is shown.
- **For whole-brain modeling:** modest classification performance can still be valuable when the model becomes a perturbable control testbed rather than just a diagnostic scoreboard.
- **For methods papers:** showing that the obvious pathology-ranked focal target fails is a stronger control than only reporting that the preferred target succeeds.
- **For closed-loop framing:** adaptive control on a disease-relevant discriminant can matter even in a slow model if it materially reduces required stimulation amplitude.
- **For today's clinical lane:** the fresh schizophrenia target-engagement trial is respectable, but connectivity change without stronger outcome logic or full-text inspection does not outrank today's better-targeting paper.
- **For hypnosis, hypnotherapy, and CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry:** the standing-interest sweep was real, but nothing fresh beat the existing archive anchors on mechanism, transfer value, or implementation logic.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful July 30 lesson is that abnormality is not the same thing as leverage. Today's preserve earns that lesson by making the bad targeting heuristic fail inside the same generative model that proposes the better one. The strongest part is not the Alzheimer's classifier. It is the explicit comparison showing that the most-affected nodes are poor focal control handles, while patient-specific discriminant-aligned cortical targets can move the modeled system much more effectively.
