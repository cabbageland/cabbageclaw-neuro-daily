## Short overview

The new preserve is the August 2, 2026 arXiv paper **EpiLENS: Patient-Relative Epileptogenic Zone Localization from Multi-Center Intracranial EEG**. It is the strongest fresh keep because it takes a problem cabbageland actually cares about, interventional localization under ugly heterogeneity, and fixes the framing before it decorates the architecture. Instead of asking for a universal channel classifier across pooled intracranial EEG, it asks whether a contact is abnormal relative to the patient's own implanted set, then combines a primary patient-relative probability model with a conservative auxiliary ranking model focused on boundary contacts and recovery of the full epileptogenic set.

That choice pays off in the right places. On 80 postoperative seizure-free patients from four centers, spanning 256 seizures and 7,635 implanted channels, the fused CDEL model reaches **Macro-F1 0.6371**, **EZ-F1 0.4485**, and **AUROC 0.7468** with near-zero epileptogenic-fraction bias. Relative to the strongest baseline, logistic regression with patient-wise z-score normalization, it improves Macro-F1 by **0.0306** and EZ-F1 by **0.0497**. Relative to its own primary branch, it still adds **0.0089** Macro-F1 and **0.0186** EZ-F1, which matters because it shows the auxiliary boundary-ranking logic is not decorative. The paper also survives harder transfer tests better than the usual raw-iEEG baselines: in leave-one-center-out evaluation it posts the best center-mean Macro-F1 at **0.6163** and the best worst-center score at **0.5773**, while cross-seizure aggregation rises from **0.6146** with one seizure to **0.6395** when all available seizures are used in the matched 73-patient subset.

The caveats are important and manageable. The labels are retrospective clinician-defined surgical-target surrogates rather than direct biological truth. The cohort includes only seizure-free postoperative patients, which cleans supervision but narrows the case mix. The inputs are still engineered spectral and waveform features rather than a richer multimodal state estimate. So this is not a turnkey surgical planner. But it is a serious paper because it makes the decision frame less stupid: localize relative abnormality within the patient, then spend extra supervision on the channels that are hardest to separate and most costly to miss.

The runner-up methods paper, **Design and Validation of a Portable EEG–tES Platform Supporting High-Rate EEG Recording and Temporal Interference Stimulation**, is worth remembering but not preserving today. Its engineering claim is solid enough: an MCU-centered device handles 8-channel EEG up to 8 kHz plus 2-channel stimulation, records concurrent temporal-interference carriers, and keeps measured loop delays under 1 millisecond. The problem is not that it is fake. The problem is that the validation remains bench and gelatine-phantom only, with no human data and no actual adaptive control algorithm yet. That makes it a promising platform paper, not today's canonical note.

## Most relevant paper

**EpiLENS: Patient-Relative Epileptogenic Zone Localization from Multi-Center Intracranial EEG**

The useful move here is not "deep learning for epilepsy" by itself. The paper earns attention because it rejects the lazy assumption that a globally learned channel score should mean the same thing across patients, seizures, implants, and centers. Instead it asks whether a contact is abnormal relative to the patient's own implanted set, then uses that patient-relative evidence as the main coordinate system.

That framing is coupled to a sensible division of labor. PRQ-Net is the main classifier, designed to capture patient-relative epileptogenic evidence that recurs across seizures while preserving lower-tail events that are strong but intermittent. BCR-Net is not allowed to become a second pseudo-classifier that muddies calibration. It is trained separately to improve ranking around ambiguous EZ versus NEZ boundaries and coverage of the full epileptogenic set. The final fusion is fixed and conservative at `0.8 * PRQ + 0.2 * BCR`, which is exactly the sort of humility more clinical-ML papers could use.

The results are not magic but they are real. On the 80-patient split, CDEL beats the strongest baseline, logistic regression with patient-wise z-score normalization, by **0.0306 Macro-F1** and **0.0497 EZ-F1**, while keeping epileptogenic-fraction bias near zero. It also improves over PRQ-Net alone, which matters because it means the boundary-ranking branch is adding patient-specific structure rather than generic smoothing. The leave-one-center-out result is especially useful for judgment: the method keeps the best center-average and worst-center Macro-F1 under held-out center transfer, which is a better stress test than flattering one pooled split.

What it does not solve is equally clear. The target labels are still retrospective surgical-target surrogates, not ground-truth biological EZ. The cohort is restricted to seizure-free postoperative cases. The features are engineered rather than truly multimodal. So this is not yet a surgical recommender. It is a strong paper about how to pose the localization problem more sanely under heterogeneity.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For epilepsy localization framing:** the paper argues that channel abnormality should be judged relative to the patient's own implanted set, not only by a universal cross-patient threshold.
- **For model design:** it shows a cleaner separation between calibrated primary evidence and auxiliary boundary-ranking evidence than most multitask clinical papers manage.
- **For evaluation discipline:** it uses patient-wise splits, validation-only threshold selection, boundary-channel analysis, cross-seizure aggregation, and leave-one-center-out transfer rather than one flattering pooled number.
- **For seizure-intervention logic:** it makes the hard contacts, boundary channels, incomplete coverage, intermittent evidence, more explicit targets for future stimulation or resection planning.
- **For future baselines:** epilepsy-localization papers should probably have to beat patient-relative feature baselines, not only global raw-signal models that collapse under heterogeneity.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 16 lesson is that some localization problems should stop pretending to be universal classifiers. Today's preserve matters because it reframes epileptogenic-zone localization as a patient-relative inference problem, then shows that this framing improves balanced performance, ambiguous-boundary ranking, cross-seizure aggregation, and cross-center transfer on multi-center intracranial EEG. It is still retrospective and label-limited. But it gives a better decision vocabulary for intervention-adjacent localization than most raw-signal deep models do.
