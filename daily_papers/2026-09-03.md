## Short overview

The most useful fresh preserve today is **Design and Validation of a Portable EEG–tES Platform Supporting High-Rate EEG Recording and Temporal Interference Stimulation**. It wins because it forces a very basic question that a lot of neuromodulation rhetoric would prefer not to answer: can the device actually stimulate and sense at the same time without clipping, hiding the carrier, or quietly outsourcing the hard part to bulky lab hardware? Here the answer is usefully yes, at least at the platform-validation level.

The paper is not glamorous, and that is exactly the point. It integrates **8-channel EEG acquisition**, **2-channel tES**, **up to 8 kHz sampling**, and programmable **tDCS**, **tACS**, and **tTIS** in a single MCU-centered wearable stack. The results are the kind that matter for future control rather than for slide decks: **99 percent** correlation in direct cable EEG injection, **93.5 percent** correlation in a gelatine head phantom, **sub-1 percent current error** across the tested stimulation modes, raw concurrent EEG-stimulation recordings without clipping, **CPU load below 53 percent** in the hardest tested mode, and **sub-millisecond synchronization** between sensing and stimulation onset. That does not give you a biomarker, a controller, or a treatment. It does give you a less fake systems baseline.

The best existing comparison anchors are already in the archive. **Efficient artifact removal for adaptive deep brain stimulation and a temporal event localization analysis** remains the right downstream reminder that preserving the mixed signal is only step one; somebody still has to clean it fast enough for adaptive use. **Direct delivery of modulated kilohertz frequency waveforms enable simultaneous electrical stimulation and recording with minimal-artifact** remains the best waveform-level companion, because it attacks observability from the signal-design side rather than the board-design side. **A Whole-Brain Dynamical Framework Linking Resting-State Activity to TMS-Evoked Responses** remains the better computational anchor for what all this plumbing is supposed to support: an intervention model with constrained perturbation logic rather than a nicer piece of hardware theater.

The fresh clinical and concept lanes were worth checking but not worth preserving above the platform paper. The strongest fresh clinical surface was **Hippocampal Dentate Gyrus Plasticity and Its Association With Dimensional Clinical Improvement Across Four Depression Treatment Interventions**, whose abstract is actually useful because it pushes against the lazy "shared antidepressant plasticity" story: dentate-gyrus volume increase seems mainly driven by **ECT**, not **CBT**, **pharmacotherapy**, or **rTMS**. But it is still an **abstract-only** read in a **prospective nonrandomized** design, so it does not beat a full-text methods keep. The freshest concept piece, **Slow-Fast Brain-Computer Interfaces: Preventing Neuroadaptive Overfitting in AI-Mediated Neural Interfaces**, has a good instinct about proxy optimization and agency, but it is still a commentary framework rather than a mechanistic or empirical preserve.

The standing-interest checks also stayed in check. In the **CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry** lane, **Efficacy of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation combined with cognitive behavioral therapy for depression: a systematic review and meta-analysis** is cleaner than generic synergy mush because it restricts its quantitative comparison to **rTMS-based controls** and reports a **small-to-moderate pooled benefit**. But it is still mostly implementation guidance, not a sharper mechanism paper than the existing combination-treatment anchor. In the **hypnosis / hypnotherapy** lane, **Magnetoencephalography monitoring of hypnosis-induced modulation of event-related brain responses by hypnosis** is respectable because it at least asks for a physiological marker, but the signal is basically auditory-response attenuation in **20 volunteers**, and the abstract says hypnosis and distraction are more similar than dramatically different. That is useful citation material, not a better state-change note than the archive hypnosis preserve.

## Most relevant paper

**Design and Validation of a Portable EEG–tES Platform Supporting High-Rate EEG Recording and Temporal Interference Stimulation**

This is the most relevant paper today because it picks the right fight. Too much closed-loop neuromodulation writing assumes the sensing stream will somehow be there when needed, as if the device can stimulate hard, observe honestly, and synchronize tightly by default. This paper does not solve the whole problem, but it does at least build around the problem instead of around the demo.

The system design is concrete enough to matter. The platform combines an **STM32 MCU**, an **ADS1299 eight-channel EEG front-end**, and a **dual-channel DAC plus Howland current-source stack** that can produce **tDCS**, **tACS**, **tTIS**, and ramping profiles. The sensing side runs from **250 Hz to 8 kHz per channel**, which is the key move because it allows the system to capture not just slow EEG but the stimulation-contaminated broadband mess, including **kilohertz-range tTIS carriers**. The waveform-generation side uses a dual-buffer DDS scheme so the MCU can keep generating the next segment while the previous one is being streamed, which is a practical way to keep the system embedded instead of quietly requiring FPGA-class hardware.

The results are properly infrastructural. Cable-injected EEG reaches **99 percent** correlation with the reference waveform. A more realistic gelatine-head-phantom test still reaches **93.5 percent**. Across tested **tDCS**, **tACS**, and **tTIS** settings, current-amplitude error stays below **1 percent**. Raw concurrent recordings show the expected mixed-signal structure and spectral peaks without clipping, which matters because a clipped signal is useless for later artifact modeling no matter how portable the board looks. On the systems side, startup latency stays around **1.56 to 1.67 seconds**, **CPU load stays under 53 percent** even for eight channels at **8 kHz** with concurrent **2000/2040 Hz tTIS**, battery-powered BLE operation draws **139.3 mW** for EEG-only and **249.6 mW** for EEG plus **2 mA tACS**, and sensing-stimulation synchronization stays below **1 ms**.

The reason this is only a platform keep, not a victory lap, is that the missing parts are still the important parts. There are no human recordings, no online artifact-recovery method, no adaptive policy, and no evidence that the preserved mixed signal can be turned into a trustworthy state estimate during active stimulation. The authors also note hardware support up to **4 mA**, which is acceptable as a research-capacity statement but still a good reminder that platform papers like to imply future flexibility before they prove physiological use. So the value here is not that closed-loop tES is solved. The value is that the paper raises the baseline for what an honest portable platform should have to show before anyone starts promising personalized control.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For closed-loop tES:** this raises the baseline from "can it stimulate?" to "can it preserve the mixed sensing-plus-stimulation signal with explicit power, latency, and sync budgets?"
- **For temporal interference work:** direct capture of kilohertz carriers matters because otherwise a lot of tTIS monitoring and artifact-modeling claims are just hand-waving around unseen waveforms.
- **For adaptive neuromodulation:** today's keep pairs naturally with the archive SMARTA+ note, because observability and cleanup are separate bottlenecks and both have to be real.
- **For intervention modeling:** the right destination is still something like the archive whole-brain TMS-evoked framework, where sensing and perturbation are tied to a constrained explanatory model rather than only to a wearable form factor.
- **For clinical translation:** the fresh dentate-gyrus plasticity paper is interesting precisely because it argues against a shared antidepressant mechanism, but an abstract-only, nonrandomized comparison is still not enough to outrank a full-text preserve.
- **For CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry:** the fresh rTMS-plus-CBT meta-analysis supports an additive signal, but it still reads more like implementation narrowing than like a mechanism breakthrough.
- **For hypnosis and hypnotherapy:** the fresh MEG paper is respectable, but if hypnosis looks almost like distraction in the key physiological comparison, then the stronger archive note still owns the state-change slot.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful September third lesson is that a device does not become closed loop just because it has both electrodes and software. Today's preserve matters because it treats simultaneous stimulation and sensing as a real systems constraint with measurable tradeoffs in bandwidth, artifact exposure, synchronization, and compute headroom. That is a much better scientific habit than calling every wearable stimulator "adaptive" and hoping the recording problem will solve itself later.
