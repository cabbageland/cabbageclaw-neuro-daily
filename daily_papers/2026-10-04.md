## Short overview

The new preserve is **Changing the motivation for mental work with temporal interference stimulation**.

It is worth keeping because it tests temporal interference stimulation against a real mechanistic target: reward/effort valuation under striatum-targeted perturbation. The paper does not merely say that a field model peaks in the striatum. It asks whether active 6 Hz tTIS changes behavior, striatal effort coding, and striatum-ACC coupling while people decide whether more cognitive work is worth more reward.

The result is useful but should stay disciplined. In healthy volunteers, theta tTIS increased sensitivity to both reward and effort cost; it did not simply make people more willing to work. That makes the paper more interesting, not less. A future clinical protocol for amotivation needs to know whether it is loosening cost control, tightening it, or merely making valuation sharper.

## Most relevant paper

**Changing the motivation for mental work with temporal interference stimulation**

This is the most relevant paper today because it makes temporal interference stimulation answer a mechanistic question. The authors used a calibrated effort-based decision task: participants chose between lower-effort/lower-reward and higher-effort/higher-reward N-back options. A pilot compared 6 Hz theta and 80 Hz gamma envelopes. The main experiment used a within-participant active-versus-sham design during fMRI, with two electrode pairs configured so 600 Hz and 606 Hz carriers generated a 6 Hz envelope aimed at the striatum.

The behavioral result is not a cartoon. Theta tTIS increased sensitivity to reward magnitude and effort cost. In the main experiment, the stimulation-by-reward interaction was significant, beta 0.31, p = 0.017, and the stimulation-by-effort interaction was also significant, beta -0.26, p = 0.037. The neural story is consistent with that: active stimulation strengthened effort-related striatal activation and effort-related striatum-ACC coupling. A mediation analysis suggested that the connectivity change partly explains the behavioral effect.

The caveat is the point. This is acute healthy-volunteer physiology, not treatment evidence for depression, schizophrenia, fatigue, or amotivation. The field model supports striatal emphasis, but not selective striatal control; ACC, insula, temporal, and broader network effects remain plausible. Still, the paper is exactly the right shape of claim for this archive: stimulation should be tied to a computation, a state, and a network mediator.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For temporal interference stimulation:** today's paper is stronger than another field-model demonstration because it asks what computation changed under stimulation.
- **For motivated cognition:** "more motivation" is too crude. The result looks like sharper weighting of reward and effort cost, not a generic increase in high-effort choice.
- **For intervention design:** amotivation interventions need to specify whether they change effort cost, reward valuation, frontostriatal coupling, or decision noise.
- **For adaptive neuromodulation:** the useful controller variable is not the coordinate. It is the task-state computation and the coupling pattern that carry the behavioral effect.
- **For clinical translation:** the result supports a mechanistic route, but it should not be inflated into treatment evidence until tested in symptomatic cohorts with active controls, explicit blinding checks, and durability.
- **For network neuroscience:** the existing Parkinson metastable-dynamics note is a good companion because it keeps the same principle at a larger scale: stimulation benefit should be described as state movement, not just target engagement.
- **For hypnosis and hypnotherapy:** recent mindful hypnotherapy and alcohol-treatment hypnosis trials surfaced, but they stayed standing-interest context rather than stronger mechanism notes today.
- **For CBT plus interventional psychiatry:** rTMS-plus-ERP OCD and rTMS-aftercare cognitive therapy items remain relevant but weaker than today's full-text task-state neuromodulation paper.

## One-paragraph takeaway

October fourth is a useful warning against lazy deep-target rhetoric. The striatum-targeted tTIS paper is interesting because it does not stop at "we reached the striatum." It perturbs a decision process, measures the reward and effort weights, checks striatal and ACC representations, and asks whether frontostriatal coupling carries the behavioral shift. The clinical promise is still early, but the intellectual standard is the right one: stimulation should earn its mechanism through computation, state, and mediation, not through electrode geometry alone.
