## Most relevant paper

**Assessing the validity and reliability of computational phenotyping of motivation**

Jaffré and colleagues jointly infer item values from ratings and choices, then separately infer incentive, effort-cost, and fatigue parameters from handgrip performance. Preference dimension means recover better than between-item dispersion. Performance reward/effort estimates are more stable than fatigue, but preference and performance estimates do not significantly correlate across participants. All task incentives were hypothetical, not paid earnings. The study supports a parameter-by-parameter measurement audit; it does not establish a common neural motivation variable, patient treatment-response sensitivity, or an intervention target.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **Novelty:** Psychometric characterization of existing models, not a new circuit mechanism or stimulation method. Do not relabel parameter identifiability as causal identification.
- **Baseline:** Include raw/model-free behavioral measures and questionnaires. The reported lack of model-based reliability improvement is an important negative result, even though jointly inferred values generally help predict held-out choices.
- **Framing:** Distinguish simulation recovery, across-person temporal stability, within-person change, and sensitivity to clinically meaningful intervention effects. None automatically supplies the next.
- **Control:** Recovering a latent quantity does not show that a stimulation input can steer it. The DBS note provides clinical state-tracking context; the control note makes the missing input map explicit.
- **Heterogeneity:** A nonsignificant group visit effect does not certify individual stability. Model comparison favors individual effort/fatigue variation even when the average change cancels out.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The steal-worthy workflow is to audit the proposed state before optimizing an intervention around it: recover its parameters under realistic noise, expose calibration and scale ambiguity, quantify individual uncertainty across repeated baselines, compare with simple summaries, and then test change under a randomized intervention against an independent outcome. Today's motivation paper clears parts of that chain for selected measures, not the whole chain. That makes it useful precisely because it narrows the claim rather than decorating a task coefficient with a circuit story.
