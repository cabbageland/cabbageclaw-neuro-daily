## Short overview

The fresh preserve today is the July 23, 2026 arXiv preprint **Foundation Models for EEG Are Blind to Long-Range Temporal Correlations: A Spectral-Temporal Dissociation Behind Their Cross-Population Fragility**. It wins because it makes a vague complaint concrete. Instead of merely saying that EEG foundation models struggle across sites, the paper asks what clinically meaningful signal the frozen embedding may have already thrown away. The answer is ugly and useful. Across five models, the embeddings carry static spectrum and recording-site identity far better than they carry the long-range temporal structure of the alpha envelope, even though that temporal feature is exactly the sort of dimensionless thing that should have a better chance of surviving cohort shifts. When a representation decodes site at 0.98 to 1.00 balanced accuracy and still cannot recover the target dynamical quantity, that is not a minor nuisance. That is the problem.

The paper is strongest where it refuses to stop at the headline. Spectral-input models such as CBraMod and BIOT recover the 1/f slope strongly, with R² values around 0.59 to 0.73, but they still fail to recover the DFA target consistently across cohorts. The classical handcrafted baseline does better on the actual temporal target, and the paper estimates a reliability ceiling instead of pretending any target has a ceiling of 1.0 just because the symbol R² allows it. It then adds the right controls: ordered-versus-shuffled pre-pool tests, residualization against 1/f, leak-aware handling of pretraining corpora, and explicit site-decoding analyses. The net result is a real mechanism claim, not just benchmark melancholy. Current frozen EEG foundation-model embeddings often preserve the static shape of the signal and the identity of the recording environment better than the temporal dynamics a biomarker program would actually want to carry forward.

## Most relevant paper

**Foundation Models for EEG Are Blind to Long-Range Temporal Correlations: A Spectral-Temporal Dissociation Behind Their Cross-Population Fragility**

This is the most relevant paper because it says something sharper than "foundation models are not magic." It asks what they are actually preserving, and then measures the answer on a target that matters. The target is the detrended-fluctuation-analysis exponent of the alpha-band envelope, which is dimensionless, partly site-robust, and defined by temporal order. That makes it a good stress test for any embedding that claims to support cross-site biomarker work.

The results are not flattering. The spectral-input models recover 1/f well, with R² values around 0.59 to 0.73 depending on cohort, but no model recovers the temporal target consistently across cohorts. The classical baseline gets to R² = 0.32 on CAUEEG and 0.38 on BrainLat, against a measured CAUEEG reliability ceiling of R² = 0.64. Meanwhile, site is decodable from the frozen embeddings at balanced accuracy 0.98 to 1.00, while the simple DFA feature is much more site-robust. That is a very specific indictment. These embeddings are often better at remembering where the recording came from than at preserving the scale-free temporal property a downstream clinical user might actually care about.

The controls matter just as much as the headline. Ordered-versus-shuffled pre-pool tests show that the weak DFA signal some models recover is not meaningfully order-dependent in the way real LRTC should be. Residualization against 1/f shows the target is not merely a static spectral shadow. And the paper does not pretend its transfer arm is stronger than it is. The frozen REVE embedding collapses to chance on the tested Western-versus-Korean transfer directions, whereas the raw DFA feature trends above chance across all tested directions but does not survive family-wise correction. Good. That is exactly the right amount of honesty.

The main caveat is that this is still a preprint diagnosis paper, not a completed remedy. The proposed LRTC-aware auxiliary loss is interesting, but it is still a prescription, not a demonstrated rescue. Even so, the paper earns a preserve because it forces a better standard. If an EEG foundation model is going to be used as a frozen biomarker or intervention backbone, it should be audited for the slow temporal structure it keeps, not merely celebrated because it performs decently within site.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For EEG foundation-model evaluation:** site decoding near ceiling should be treated as failure evidence, not as a side curiosity.
- **For biomarker baselines:** a simple dimensionless dynamical feature can be a stronger cold-start transfer baseline than a large frozen embedding.
- **For representation audits:** separating static-spectrum recovery from temporal-order recovery is more informative than reporting one blended downstream score.
- **For pretraining design:** if long-range temporal structure matters, the objective has to reward it explicitly instead of hoping reconstruction or contrastive patch learning picks it up accidentally.
- **For neuropsychiatric transfer claims:** cross-cohort success should be benchmarked against reliability ceilings and explicit site-leakage tests, not only against internal validation.
- **For today's fresh clinical lane:** the OCD subtype DBS preprint, accelerated iTBS reward-circuit paper, and pediatric thalamic neuromodulation case series were real checks, but none beat today's methods paper or the archive anchors on mechanistic sharpness.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 1 lesson is that representation quality is not the same thing as benchmark glamour. A frozen EEG foundation model that preserves site and static spectrum while discarding slow temporal structure is not a general backbone. It is a site-entangled spectral compressor. Today's preserve matters because it makes that failure measurable and therefore fixable, or at least falsifiable, before more biomarker and neuromodulation work gets built on top of it.
