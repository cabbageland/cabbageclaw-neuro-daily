## Short overview

The best new preserve today is **Modulating task-outcome value to mitigate real-world procrastination via noninvasive brain stimulation**. It is full-text open-access eLife work, and it earns the note because it does something intervention papers too often skip: it names a candidate computation, perturbs a plausible prefrontal node, and samples the proposed computation repeatedly around actual behavior.

The paper is not a clinical breakthrough. Forty-six high-procrastination adults were randomized to active or sham high-definition tDCS over left DLPFC across seven sessions. On alternating task days, they reported real pending tasks, sampled task aversiveness and task-outcome value, and documented task completion. Active stimulation increased task-execution willingness, reduced actual procrastination rates, and left a smaller effect at six-month follow-up. The sharpest mechanistic claim is that increased task-outcome value, not reduced task aversiveness, statistically tracked the behavioral improvement.

That is useful because it gives future CBT-plus-neuromodulation and interventional-psychiatry work a concrete design principle. Do not say "executive control" and wander off. Specify whether the intervention is changing aversiveness, expected value, temporal discounting, effort cost, cue salience, or some other control variable, then measure that variable close to the real action.

The caveat is large enough to matter. The active group's short-term procrastination rate reportedly collapses to 0 percent, which is cleaner than one should expect from tDCS in real humans. eLife's public review also flags sample size and generalization. So the preserve is not "DLPFC stimulation fixes procrastination." The preserve is "this is a better-shaped intervention experiment than the usual prefrontal stimulation mush, and its effect-size drama is exactly why replication is mandatory."

The standing-interest checks did not beat the eLife preserve. Hypnosis and hypnotherapy surfaced clinical hypnosis for migraine, functional neurological disorder/suggestion/VR, ego-strengthening hypnotherapy and suicidal thoughts, hypnosis effects on sensory decoding, and predictive-coding framing for mindfulness and hypnotic susceptibility. CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry surfaced tDCS plus CBT for low back pain in veterans, ketamine-assisted psychotherapy for chronic neuropathic pain, and tDCS added to cognitive-behavioral group therapy for tobacco dependence. Useful lanes, but today's best full-text mechanism note is the value-sampling neuromodulation paper.

## Most relevant paper

**Modulating task-outcome value to mitigate real-world procrastination via noninvasive brain stimulation**

This is the most relevant paper today because it links noninvasive stimulation to a specific behavioral-control variable. Procrastination is easy to flatten into "bad self-control." This paper instead asks whether repeated left-DLPFC HD-tDCS shifts the tradeoff between task aversiveness and task-outcome value, then measures those quantities repeatedly around real tasks.

The method is unusually usable for this lane. Participants named real next-day tasks, rated aversiveness and outcome value through an experience-sampling app, and reported completion at the deadline with supporting evidence. The stimulation protocol was seven sessions of 2.0 mA anodal HD-tDCS over left DLPFC, with sham controls and follow-up. The authors modeled task-execution willingness, actual procrastination rate, aversiveness, outcome value, and exploratory mediation.

The result is interesting and over-clean at the same time. Active stimulation improved willingness and reduced procrastination more than sham, with a smaller effect still visible at six months. Increased task-outcome value was the variable that tracked improvement; reduced task aversiveness did not do the same work. But the active group's short-term procrastination rate going to 0 percent is a replication siren. Treat this as a design pattern and mechanistic hypothesis, not as settled treatment evidence.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For neuromodulation-plus-behavior designs:** measure the proposed psychological control variable, not only the distal behavior.
- **For CBT augmentation:** the useful combination question is whether therapy changes the content of value and stimulation changes the gain or stability of that value signal.
- **For DLPFC targeting:** "executive control" is too vague; future work should separate value, effort, temporal discounting, working memory, attentional maintenance, and aversive avoidance.
- **For real-world behavioral outcomes:** ecological task sampling is worth copying, but task difficulty and participant-selected goals need tighter modeling.
- **For causal claims:** exploratory mediation should be treated as pathway prioritization, not proof.
- **For replication:** unusually large tDCS effects should trigger larger multi-site tests and responder modeling before clinical translation.
- **For hypnosis/hypnotherapy:** recent sensory-decoding and predictive-coding papers keep pointing toward state modulation and precision weighting, but today's strongest full-text intervention logic came from tDCS.
- **For CBT-plus-interventional psychiatry:** the tDCS-plus-CBT and ketamine-assisted psychotherapy hits are exactly the right lane, but they need mechanism-specific combination logic rather than therapeutic stacking.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The September eighteenth lesson is that serious intervention design has to name the thing it is trying to move. Today's eLife preserve does not prove that HD-tDCS is a practical procrastination treatment, but it does give a better template: perturb a plausible circuit, sample the candidate cognitive variable near real behavior, compare rival pathways, and keep the causal language disciplined. For cabbageland, the steal-worthy move is not procrastination treatment. It is value-pathway measurement as the bridge between behavioral therapy and neuromodulation.
