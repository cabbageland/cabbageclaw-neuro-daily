## Short overview

The new preserve is **Excitation-Inhibition Balance and Fronto-Limbic Connectivity Distinguish TMS Treatment Outcomes in Refractory Depression**.

It is worth keeping because it tries to make depression iTBS response mechanistically legible. The authors combine TMS-EEG with connectome-based Jansen-Rit whole-brain modeling in 90 treatment-resistant depression patients, then ask which fitted physiological and state-space features separate responders from non-responders. The signal is not just "TMS changed the EEG." Responders show stronger post-treatment suppression of 3-10 Hz induced power, model-inferred shifts toward inhibitory control, baseline DLPFC-sgACC state structure, and post-treatment trajectory stabilization.

The right read is disciplined optimism. This is not a clinical decision rule yet: the analysis lacks sham control, uses normative structural connectivity, and depends on a simplified neural mass model. But it is a much better frame than another loose biomarker list. It says that personalized TMS should estimate who is in a reachable frontolimbic state, what physiological mechanism the stimulation changed, and whether the perturbation actually reorganized the patient's dynamics.

## Most relevant paper

**Excitation-Inhibition Balance and Fronto-Limbic Connectivity Distinguish TMS Treatment Outcomes in Refractory Depression**

This is the most relevant paper today because it puts the depression-TMS biomarker story on firmer mechanistic ground. The authors start with a familiar clinical problem: left DLPFC iTBS helps some patients with treatment-resistant depression, but response remains variable enough that "standard target plus standard protocol" is still too blunt. Their move is to treat TMS-EEG as a perturbational readout and then fit a whole-brain Jansen-Rit model to translate evoked responses into candidate circuit mechanisms.

The main result is convergent rather than singular. Responders show larger post-treatment reductions in 3-10 Hz induced power. Model fitting and simulations interpret that low-frequency suppression as a shift toward inhibitory control, especially reduced pyramidal-to-excitatory drive and increased inhibitory-to-pyramidal feedback. Separately, a pre-treatment model-derived state distinguishes responders from non-responders, with opposite-polarity DLPFC and sgACC loadings. That matters because it turns the familiar DLPFC-sgACC anticorrelation literature into a perturbational, model-derived state feature instead of another static targeting slogan.

The trajectory analysis is the most cabbageland-shaped piece. Responders show greater treatment-induced state-space reorganization and closer convergence to the model's stable point after treatment. That does not prove the attractor story is literally the clinical mechanism, but it usefully reframes response as a dynamical-system move: stimulation works when the system is in a state where the perturbation can push it into a more stable, less pathological regime.

The caveats are not decorative. The analyzed mechanism is not sham-controlled, the connectome is normative rather than patient-specific, the fitted E/I parameters are model interpretations rather than direct synaptic measurements, and the protocol uses 1200 pulses per day. So the paper should not be promoted as a ready clinical selector. Its value is sharper: it shows what a serious TMS biomarker paper should try to do, namely combine perturbation, physiology, network state, and clinical outcome without pretending that a scalp feature alone is the mechanism.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For clinical neuromodulation:** response biomarkers should be perturbational and mechanistic, not just baseline correlates.
- **For depression TMS:** DLPFC-sgACC targeting needs to be expressed as a patient-state and circuit-reactivity problem, not only a coordinate-selection rule.
- **For TMS-EEG:** low-frequency suppression becomes more useful when tied to a model of inhibitory control rather than treated as a free-floating component.
- **For computational psychiatry:** fitted physiological parameters are useful only if the paper stays honest about their inferential status; this one mostly does.
- **For closed-loop design:** the natural controller target is not symptom score alone, but a measurable evoked-response state that moves toward responder-like dynamics.
- **For personalized targeting:** today's note should be paired with precision network mapping, because normative structural coupling is one of the paper's main translational weaknesses.
- **For baselines:** future work has to beat simple TMS-EEG components, resting-state DLPFC-sgACC connectivity, target coordinates, and clinical severity before the model earns deployment.
- **For hypnosis and hypnotherapy:** standing checks again surfaced hypnosis decoding and MEG state-biomarker material. It remains relevant to state-monitoring logic, but today the stronger preserve is the perturbational TMS paper.
- **For CBT plus interventional psychiatry:** CBT-ENDURE and ketamine/esketamine plus CBT sequencing remain useful combination-care context. Today's mechanistic lesson is that combination timing should eventually be tied to state readiness, not just calendar sequencing.

## One-paragraph takeaway

October fourteenth's useful lesson is that TMS response should be studied as a state-dependent perturbation, not as a generic treatment exposure. The new Biological Psychiatry paper is imperfect, but it makes the right kind of move: use TMS-EEG to perturb the system, use a physiologically interpretable whole-brain model to estimate what changed, and ask whether responder dynamics differ before and after treatment. For cabbageland, the steal-worthy frame is to separate three questions: who is in a reachable state, which mechanism the stimulation actually changed, and whether the post-treatment trajectory moved into a more stable circuit regime.
