## Short overview

The fresh preserve today is the August 4, 2026 *Human Brain Mapping* paper **Depression Improvement Correlates With Lower TMS Intensity in a Randomized Trial With Real-Time E-Field Modeling**. It wins because it goes after a neglected but clinically actionable lever: dose. A lot of depression TMS still talks as if `120% rMT` were a meaningful unit of cortical stimulation rather than a machine-output convention inherited from old protocol drift. This paper makes the problem legible. In 28 participants from a randomized, double-blind accelerated iTBS trial with usable MRI, the authors used real-time E-field guidance to match left DLPFC stimulation to motor-equivalent field strength, then checked that decision against subject-specific finite-element modeling. Required DLPFC intensity ranged from 49.7% to 150.4% of resting motor threshold. Real-time guidance improved precision against the motor-equivalent target by 48.1% relative to a flat 120% rule. And the most interesting result is the uncomfortable one: better depression improvement tracked *lower* DLPFC field strength, while delivered `%rMT` itself did not track outcome.

That is a real intervention-logic contribution. It does not prove a new dosing standard. The clinical analysis is retrospective and exploratory, the sample is small, the target is anatomical rather than connectivity-guided, and there was no randomized intensity arm. But it still changes the question in a useful way. If the thing that matters is cortical field at target rather than a visible thumb twitch proxy, then a lot of "underdosed" versus "fully dosed" talk may just be category error.

## Most relevant paper

**Depression Improvement Correlates With Lower TMS Intensity in a Randomized Trial With Real-Time E-Field Modeling**

The useful move here is embarrassingly basic and therefore overdue. Depression TMS still inherits a lot of dosing talk from motor-threshold convention, as though "120 percent of resting motor threshold" were a biologically meaningful statement about what the prefrontal cortex actually receives. This paper tests that assumption by separating machine output from estimated cortical dose. The authors take a 30-participant randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled accelerated iTBS trial in major depressive disorder, drop two subjects with unusable MRI, and then ask what DLPFC stimulation intensity is needed to match motor-equivalent field strength once actual anatomy is modeled rather than guessed.

The setup has two layers. During treatment planning, a Nexstim neuronavigation system uses a real-time spherical head model to estimate E-field strength and sets DLPFC intensity to match the field recorded at M1 during motor-threshold determination. Offline, the authors then build subject-specific finite-element simulations in SimNIBS to estimate the actual cortical fields delivered under three scenarios: M1 at motor threshold, DLPFC under the conventional `120% rMT` rule, and DLPFC under the individualized real-time E-field-guided setting. They also ask which intensity description, delivered `%rMT`, DLPFC-to-M1 field ratio, or absolute DLPFC field strength, best tracks one-week change in QIDS-SR16 after the single-day accelerated course.

The results are exactly the kind of dissociation the field needs. Required DLPFC intensity spans a three-fold-ish range, from 49.7% to 150.4% rMT, with 53.6% of subjects requiring less than 100% rMT to reach motor-equivalent field strength. The individualized real-time method reduces mean absolute deviation from the motor-equivalent target from 0.293 to 0.170 and lowers root mean square error from 24.55 V/m to 12.75 V/m, which the paper summarizes as 48.1% better precision than the flat 120% rule. Then the clinical punchline lands: delivered `%rMT` is not associated with depression improvement, but both relative and absolute DLPFC field strength are negatively correlated with outcome at `rho = -0.49, P = .008`. Lower field, better symptom reduction.

That does not mean the paper has solved TMS dosing. The clinical analysis is retrospective, exploratory, and underpowered. There is no randomized intensity manipulation, only a secondary analysis layered onto a trial that was randomized on `d`-cycloserine versus placebo rather than on dose. The DLPFC target is structural/anatomical rather than individualized by connectivity. The real-time planning model is still a model, and the offline FEM stack is not direct physiological measurement either. But as a research note, it is unusually useful because it exposes a modifiable lever that is both mechanistically legible and clinically testable. If future trials confirm that lower field strength can outperform conventional higher-output dosing for at least some iTBS depression protocols, a lot of current "dose optimization" talk will have to be rewritten from scratch.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For depression TMS dosing:** `%rMT` is too blunt to stand in for cortical dose once individual anatomy is allowed to matter.
- **For precision neuromodulation:** personalized dose need not mean higher dose; the clinically better hypothesis here may be *more accurate and sometimes lower* DLPFC field strength.
- **For baseline choice:** any future dosing paper that compares two machine-output percentages without estimating field delivery is now easier to dismiss as under-instrumented.
- **For targeting logic:** the abstract-only JAMA targeting RCT says geometry matters, while today's full-text HBM paper says dose folklore matters too; the field probably needs both levers, not one.
- **For combination-treatment design:** if CBT-plus-stimulation papers want to sound mechanistic, they need to specify how therapy timing interacts with dose and target rather than treating stimulation as an undifferentiated adjunct.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 6 lesson is that better targeting does not rescue lazy dosing assumptions. Today's preserve matters because it shows how much cortical dose can vary under a fixed `%rMT` rule and because its most provocative signal points in the opposite direction from brute-force intuition: lower DLPFC field strength correlated with better depression improvement. That is not a clinical rule yet. It is a sharp enough result that future TMS precision papers should have to answer it.
