## Short overview

The most useful fresh preserve today is **Axonal delay dispersion decides whether a neuron detects an event or a sequence, and predicts cortical column diameter**. It wins because it takes an old but usually hand-waved idea, namely that conduction delays matter for temporal coding, and turns it into a sharper claim with measurable knobs. In the paper's full-text arXiv HTML, a single scalar, the dispersion of incoming axonal delays, moves a simulated population from plain event detection to order-selective sequence detection. The crossover point scales with the inter-event interval at a slope of **0.92 [0.83, 1.01]**, which is the kind of tidy mechanistic relation that actually deserves attention.

The paper keeps going instead of stopping at a cute simulation. The same delay dispersion also sets the longest codable interval and the timing precision the scheme can tolerate. The tolerated absolute timing error stays on the order of a millisecond, around **0.82 ms** at the stricter threshold and **1.46 ms** at the looser one, which is a much harsher claim than vague "precise timing matters" rhetoric. The model also proposes a scaling rule for cortical column diameter, essentially **diameter equals horizontal conduction velocity times coincidence-window width**, giving a **300-600 micrometer** band at the thalamocortical velocity they use as a reference. That is not proof, but it is a real prediction instead of a metaphor.

## Most relevant paper

**Axonal delay dispersion decides whether a neuron detects an event or a sequence, and predicts cortical column diameter**

This is the most relevant paper today because it makes a better claim than the standard delay-line nostalgia. The old story is that conduction delays can convert firing order into synchrony. Fine. What this paper asks is the sharper question: what anatomical variable decides whether a downstream neuron becomes an event detector or an order-selective sequence detector? Its answer is the dispersion of the incoming delay set, and that is a much more operational claim than generic temporal-coding admiration.

The core simulation result is simple and actually useful. With narrow delay dispersion, the model population behaves like event detectors. Sequence detectors are effectively absent. As the dispersion widens, sequence detectors emerge and overtake event detectors when the dispersion is about equal to the inter-event interval, with the crossover slope reported at **0.92 [0.83, 1.01]**. The order-selectivity check is also strong enough to care about: **190 of 192** classified sequence detectors were silent to the reversed-order control over the main range, which makes the result harder to dismiss as mere conjunctive activation.

The second result is the better one. The same dispersion that buys selectivity also sets the code's limits. The upper bound of the codable interval tracks the available delay range instead of the thresholding dynamics, and the tolerated absolute timing error stays roughly in the millisecond regime rather than scaling as a fixed percentage of conduction speed. That means the paper is not just saying delays matter. It is saying there is a hard precision and range budget implied by anatomy.

The third result is the boldest and also the most fragile. The paper argues that the same millisecond coincidence window and horizontal conduction velocity imply a column-diameter scaling rule, yielding a **300-600 micrometer** band at the cited reference velocity and remaining at least compatible with the two cortical areas for which the necessary measurements already exist. Two points are not a regression, and the paper says so. Still, it is a real scaling proposal instead of more decorative cortex geometry talk.

The reason to keep this for cabbageland is that it sharpens several intervention-facing intuitions at once. It says myelination and delay dispersion are not just transmission details. They are candidate control parameters for temporal readout, temporal binding limits, and state sensitivity. It also suggests that a lot of timing-heavy neurostimulation or network theory should stop talking as if temporal coding were a free resource once you decide to care about milliseconds.

The caveats are obvious and important. This is a theory-and-simulation paper with no recorded neural dataset as the main test bed. It abstracts away realistic dendritic geometry, ion-channel detail, ongoing cortical drive, and the fact that local recurrent circuits can make membrane integration time another meaningful source of effective delay. The column-diameter prediction is intriguing, but the empirical support is still argument plus two compatible measurements, not a proper comparative dataset.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

- **For temporal coding:** the useful novelty is not "delays can encode order." It is that one measurable dispersion parameter decides detector class, codable interval, and tolerated timing error.
- **For myelination:** this reframes myelination as a computational control knob rather than mere signal-speed housekeeping.
- **For intervention logic:** any stimulation story that depends on millisecond timing should probably say what delay dispersion and coincidence-window assumptions it is implicitly making.
- **For theory quality:** the paper earns attention because it offers falsifiable scaling relations and state-sensitivity predictions instead of stopping at a verbal mechanism.
- **For inverse or network methods:** this pairs well with the archive temporal-eigenmode source-imaging note by making the larger point that temporal structure has to be anatomically and mechanistically constrained, not simply admired.
- **For clinical translation:** today's fresh SNT and focused-ultrasound papers are interesting, but one is still partly exploratory and the other is n equals one, so neither beats the mechanistic keep.
- **For standing-interest lanes:** current-window hypnosis, hypnotherapy, and CBT-plus-interventional checks did not surface a better new preserve than the existing archive anchors, which is a perfectly acceptable outcome.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful September fourth lesson is that axonal delay dispersion is not just wiring trivia. In today's preserve it determines whether neurons act like event detectors or order-selective sequence detectors, sets the upper bound of the temporal code, and implies a harsh millisecond precision budget. That is a better framing for both basic circuit theory and intervention logic than the usual vague insistence that timing matters.
